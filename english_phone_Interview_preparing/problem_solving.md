## Problem-Solving Interview Tips

When interviewers talk about problem-solving skills, they mean the ability to deal with unexpected, challenging or complex situations in the workplace. Example questions:
*   *Give us an example of a situation when you realized that you wouldn't be able to meet the deadline. What did you do?*
*   *Tell me about a time when the requirements were gathered incorrectly or incompletely. How did you deal with it?*

**Tips:**
*   **Tip 1:** Say how you noticed the problem or when you faced it.
*   **Tip 2:** Describe how you identified the cause and how you analyzed it.
*   **Tip 3:** Say what solutions you generated and how you collaborated with the team.
*   **Tip 4:** Outline the most important steps you took to deal with the situation.
*   **Tip 5:** Explain how you evaluated the result or impact of your solution.

---

## Technical Interview Tips

> **Tip 1. Ask clarifying questions about the case**
> Don't give the answer straight away; take some time to think and ask clarifying questions. Don't be afraid to ask questions that check your understanding.

> **Tip 2. Make assumptions**
> Look for clues in the question. Remember to justify your assumptions: *"I am going to assume it's…, because ..."*. You might also want to restate the problem.

> **Tip 3. Walk the interviewer through your thinking process**
> Don't worry too much about giving the "right" answer. Guide the interviewer through your line of thinking. Don't go silent — say something like *"That's a good question"* to buy time.

## Useful Language — Problem Solving

*   **address the issue** → focus on or deal with this issue
*   **dissatisfied with the results** → not pleased, think results could be better
*   **missed the deadline** → can't finish within the agreed time frame
*   **handle a situation** → take necessary actions to deal with a situation
*   **be a bit of a problem** → be not a very big problem
*   **experience a delay** → have to wait because something took longer
*   **figure out the root cause** → understand the basic cause
*   **get on with the task** → continue doing something

### Rephrasing Drills

*   As it turned out, about 20% of the customers experienced delays with their orders. ➞ We found out that around 20% of the customers didn't receive their orders on time.
*   The research showed that around 20% of users were dissatisfied with the service. ➞ According to our research, about 20% of users thought that the service wasn't good enough.
*   I handled the situation by pointing out the benefits of the tool. ➞ Highlighting the benefits helped me deal with the situation.
*   We missed the initial deadline because the stakeholder asked us to work with new urgent requirements. ➞ The stakeholders wanted us to focus on other requirements so we didn't finish within the planned time period.
*   When we figured out the root cause of the issue, it wasn't too hard to fix it. ➞ When we understood the main reason why the problem occurred, fixing it was rather easy.

## Useful Language — Technical Interview

**Checking understanding**
- So you're asking me … right?
- Let me make sure I've got it right.
- So, I need to … Is that correct?

**Thinking out loud**
- Well, the first thing I'd probably do is [verb]
- Probably, I'd start by [verb+*ing*]
- What I'm trying to say is …
- Another thing I would consider is [noun/verb+*ing*]
- In addition, … / Finally, …
- Erm, what else?
- So that's basically how I would …

**Asking for feedback**
- Does that make sense?
- Does that sound OK?

**Useful phrases**
- Well, the thing is, … → In fact, …
- In a nutshell, … → Let me say it in a few words.
- Just to give an example. → Let me give you an example.
- So, to recap, … → So, to sum up, …
- For instance, … → For example, …
- As I see it, … → From my point of view, …
- I'd say that … → I think that …
- That's a tough question. → That's a difficult question.
- Let me think about this for a second. → Let me just take a moment to consider your question.
- Well, off the top of my head, I'd say that … → What comes to mind right away is that …

**More phrases**
- It all boils down to one single problem.
- The heart of the matter is that we don't have enough information.
- I can't underscore enough how important it is.
- How does this look to you?
- What support do you need?
- What would you add or change?

## Dialog Example — Technical Case Interview

**Interviewer:** An online marketplace company has introduced a new feature that allows potential buyers to start an audio chat with the seller before paying for the item. How would you measure the efficiency of the new feature?

**Lucas:** So you're asking me to what extent a newly introduced audio chats feature has positively influenced the buyer's decision to make a transaction, right?

**Interviewer:** Exactly, and we're taking into consideration only the buyer metrics.

**Lucas:** Hmm, does this data represent both periods: before and after the feature launch, or only after the launch?

**Interviewer:** Good question, Lucas. It includes both periods.

**Lucas:** Well, off the top of my head I'd say that I'd opt for conducting an A/B test since the comparison of data before and after the launch may be affected by seasonality, market conditions or some other external factors. If we have neither a controlled group nor a historical period, then the estimation of feature effectiveness could be biased… inaccurate, you know.

**Interviewer:** Right, fair enough. Although the A/B test hasn't been run, let's still compare the data before and after the change considering the dynamics only.

**Lucas:** I got it, thanks. Well, the first thing I'd probably do is check how many people have used the call option, and it only makes sense to compare the customers who have seen this feature with those who haven't. What I'm trying to say is that this feature might be useful, but if it's being used by only one percent of people, it won't affect the overall number. On the other hand, it may turn out that it's popular with the loyal customers only. And if so, then it's wrong to compare them with the historical period, since it includes both user types.

**Interviewer:** All right, valid point. Let's assume that there's no difference between the types.

**Lucas:** Sure. Another thing I would consider is checking whether there's been a change, at least on average, between the conversion before the change was implemented and after it. In addition, I would have a look at the metrics split by item category, platform, and language. Then, finally, I would also look at whether the ARPU check has increased on average. Erm, what else? Do you use some special metrics to measure the efficiency?

**Interviewer:** I'm ok with the conversion and average check metrics.

**Lucas:** Then it'd be reasonable to consider how unanswered calls affect the conversion. What I'm trying to say is that answered calls increase the conversion greatly, while unanswered ones force the user to leave. In this case, we would have to focus on reducing such cases. I'd also suggest calculating a cost-benefit analysis of this feature. So that's basically how I would go about this case. Does that make sense?

## Tips on Describing and Explaining a Task Clearly

> **Tip 1:** Start by describing the current state of things and provide context for why you need this solution. Illustrate your point with an example.

**Useful language:**
- Just to give you a bit of background (on the situation), …
- The problem (we're facing here) is that …
- The situation (as it stands) is that …

> **Tip 2:** Justify why the task is necessary or important. Mention how the project would benefit from it.

**Useful language:**
- What we need is … (so that we can …)
- What's important is *[that these events should have certain parameters]*.
- This will allow us / enable us to …
- Is that something you can help with?

> **Tip 3:** Mention the deadline. Ask about blockers.

**Useful language:**
- How long do you think it will take?
- Do you see any blockers (to that)?

# Five-Day Intensive Prep for Problem-Solving Interview

**A structured case interview has never been your world — but in five days, it can be.**

This plan targets two weaknesses — problem structuring and recommendation delivery — with daily drills designed to rewire how you think out loud.

---

## Day 1: Rewire your thinking with MECE and issue trees

### Morning: Core structuring frameworks

The **MECE principle** (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive): every bucket in your problem breakdown must have zero overlap (ME) and cover all possibilities (CE).

**Six strategies for building MECE structures:**
- **Binary split ("X" and "Not X")**: Simplest guaranteed-MECE structure.
- **Mathematical formulas**: Automatically MECE. Profit = Revenue − Costs. Revenue = Price × Volume.
- **Process/sequence breakdown**: Stages in a pipeline. For ML: data collection → feature engineering → model training → validation → deployment → monitoring.
- **Segmentation by dimension**: Geography, customer segment, time horizon, product line.
- **Conceptual pairs**: Supply vs. demand, quantitative vs. qualitative, cost vs. benefit.
- **Catch-all "other" bucket**: When you can't fully enumerate.

**Example issue tree — "ML model accuracy has dropped 8% in the last quarter":**

```
Model accuracy decline
├── Data issues
│   ├── Training data quality degradation
│   ├── Data drift (distribution shift in production)
│   └── Feature pipeline failures
├── Model issues
│   ├── Model staleness (concept drift)
│   ├── Hyperparameter suboptimality
│   └── Architecture limitations for new patterns
└── Infrastructure/deployment issues
    ├── Serving environment changes
    ├── Preprocessing inconsistencies (training-serving skew)
    └── Resource constraints affecting inference
```

### Afternoon: Bulletproof 7-step process

1. **Define** the problem precisely (concrete, measurable, time-bound)
2. **Disaggregate** using logic trees (MECE)
3. **Prioritize** by pruning branches (80/20 rule)
4. **Workplan** what analyses you'd run
5. **Analyze** — run the numbers and interpret data
6. **Synthesize** — connect findings into a coherent story
7. **Communicate** — lead with recommendation, support with evidence

**Hypothesis-driven thinking** is the key mindset shift: instead of exhaustively exploring every branch, form an initial hypothesis and then seek to confirm or refute it.

### Evening practice:
Write MECE issue trees for:
1. "Customer acquisition cost has doubled in 6 months"
2. "Fraud detection model's false positive rate is too high"
3. "ML team velocity has decreased by 30% quarter-over-quarter"

## Day 2: Master communication structures

### The Pyramid Principle — top-down communication

**Synthesis formula:**
1. **Restate the question** (one sentence)
2. **State your recommendation** (one decisive sentence — no hedging)
3. **Support with 2–3 reasons** (each backed by specific evidence)
4. **Acknowledge risks and outline next steps**

**Bad example (bottom-up):**
*"So I looked at the data pipeline and found some issues with feature freshness, and then I checked the model performance metrics... so I think maybe we should retrain the model and also fix the pipeline."*

**Good example (top-down):**
*"I recommend we prioritize fixing the data pipeline before retraining the model, for three reasons. First, our feature freshness has degraded from real-time to 6-hour lag, which directly explains 60% of the accuracy decline. Second, retraining on stale features would waste 2–3 engineering weeks. Third, fixing the pipeline is a 1-week effort that also prevents future recurrences."*

**Key rules:**
- Signpost every transition: "First, I'll look at X. Then, I'll examine Y."
- **Never go silent** — narrate your thought process
- After every step, answer the **"so what?"** question
- **Never say "it depends"** without following with a recommendation

### 5-step case interview flow:
1. **Listen and clarify** (2 min) — restate problem, ask 2–3 questions
2. **Present your framework** (2 min) — build MECE structure, present it
3. **Analyze** (15–20 min) — structure math before calculating, interpret every number
4. **Synthesize** (ongoing) — connect findings back to hypothesis
5. **Recommend** (2 min) — use the synthesis formula

## Day 3: Deep-dive cases

### Case #1 — ML model prioritization

**Prompt:** 5 competing ML project proposals, capacity for 2 this quarter. How would you prioritize?

**Framework:**
```
Prioritization criteria
├── Business impact (revenue uplift, cost reduction, compliance risk)
├── Feasibility (technical complexity, data readiness, time to production)
└── Strategic alignment (OKRs, competitive differentiation, reusability)
```

### Case #2 — ML project behind schedule

**Prompt:** ML project 4 weeks behind the 12-week schedule, 6 weeks to regulatory deadline, model precision at 0.78 vs. 0.85 target. What do you do?

**Framework:**
```
Project recovery strategy
├── Scope management (MVP model, feature reduction, phased rollout)
├── Resource optimization (unblock dependencies, reallocate resources)
└── Risk mitigation (regulatory communication, fallback plan)
```

### Case #3 — Build vs. buy ML infrastructure

**Framework:**
```
Build vs. buy decision
├── Cost analysis (TCO over 3 years)
├── Capability fit (feature coverage, customization, scalability)
├── Strategic factors (core competency, speed to value, talent retention)
└── Risk factors (vendor reliability, data sovereignty, migration risk)
```

## Day 4: Advanced cases and ethical dimensions

### Case #4 — Data quality crisis during model training

18% of training data has incorrectly labeled outcomes. The model shows "surprisingly good" validation metrics.

**Key insight:** The good metrics are a **red flag** — the model learned that high-risk patterns are "safe" because the validation set has the same label corruption. This is worse than low accuracy — it's systematically biased.

### Case #5 — AI ethics and fairness

ML model for loan decisions has 23% higher false rejection rate for certain postcodes correlated with ethnic minorities. The model uses no demographic features directly.

**Key approach:** Frame as business risk, not ethics debate. EU AI Act classifies credit scoring as high-risk AI. Technical fix: identify proxy features via SHAP values, use adversarial debiasing or calibrated threshold adjustment.

## Day 5: Full simulation and polish

### Key pitfalls to avoid:
1. **Data dump** — share too many details instead of prioritizing
2. **Generic frameworks** — use recycled templates instead of bespoke structures
3. **Hedging** — say "it depends" without recommending
4. **Solving in silence** — narrate your thinking instead
5. **Bottom-up presentation** — state the answer first
6. **Ignoring "so what"** — interpret every number
7. **Treating it as solo exercise** — engage the interviewer

### Transition phrases:
- Starting: *"Let me break this into three areas..."*
- Prioritizing: *"I'd start with X because it's the highest-impact lever..."*
- Pivoting: *"That's what I see on the revenue side. Turning to costs..."*
- Synthesizing: *"So what this tells us so far is..."*
- Recommending: *"Based on this analysis, I recommend... for three reasons."*
- Handling uncertainty: *"I don't have data on X, but based on Y and Z, a reasonable estimate would be..."*

## Quick-Reference Framework Cheat Sheet

| Framework | When to use | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| **MECE issue tree** | Breaking down any problem | 3–5 branches, each with 2–3 sub-branches |
| **Profitability tree** | Revenue/cost problems | Profit = Revenue (Price × Volume) − Costs (Fixed + Variable) |
| **Hypothesis-driven** | Throughout every case | State hypothesis → test → refine → conclude |
| **ICE scoring** | Prioritizing solutions | Impact × Confidence × Ease |
| **2×2 matrix** | Comparing options | Impact vs. Feasibility |
| **Build vs. buy** | Technology decisions | Cost (TCO), Capability fit, Strategic value, Risk |
| **Pyramid Principle** | Every recommendation | Answer → reasons → evidence → risks → next steps |
| **Bulletproof 7-step** | Complex open-ended cases | Define → Disaggregate → Prioritize → Workplan → Analyze → Synthesize → Communicate |

## Problem Solving Interview — Candidate Prep Guide

### Why is problem solving important?

The "Think Deeper" value encourages team members to approach challenges with a critical eye and an unwavering determination to dive deeper into issues to uncover unique solutions.

### What to expect at the interview?

The problem is a hypothetical challenge inspired by genuine obstacles. There are no right or wrong answers — the goal is to gauge your ability to analyse the issue, prioritise actions, and articulate your proposed solutions.

### Core Values

**Never Settle** · **Dream Team** · **Think Deeper** · **Get It Done** · **Deliver Wow**

**Never Settle** — Relentlessly push to become number one. Challenge solutions from all angles. Show initiative, inspire others. Always think beyond the task at hand. Invite criticism and alternative views.

**Get It Done** — Own your work end-to-end. Look for answers and solutions, not excuses. Bring a can-do attitude. Unblock roadblocks. Deliver on commitments.

**Deliver Wow** — Put customer first. Focus on every single detail. Keep it simple. Use language everyone can easily understand. Lead with the most important information. Bottom line up front.
