# Unit 28 — must and can't

# Theory

## A. must and can't for deductions about the present

We use **must** to say that we believe something is **certain** – the evidence leads us to one conclusion:

- Your flat is right above a nightclub. It **must be** noisy at weekends.
- You've been on your feet since six this morning. You **must be** exhausted.
- "Ravi never stops talking about football." "Ravi? You **must be joking** – he hates sport."
- Lena **must get** lonely in that job. She works alone all day.

We use **can't** to say that we believe something is **not possible**:

- You had a huge breakfast an hour ago. You **can't be** hungry already.
- They only moved in last week. They **can't know** the neighbours yet.

Structure:

| Subject | Modal | Verb form | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| I / you / she / they … | **must** / **can't** | **be** + adjective/noun | She **must be** at work. |
| | | **be -ing** | You **can't be** serious. / He **must be joking**. |
| | | other infinitive | They **must know** each other. / He **can't earn** much. |

## B. must have / can't have for the past

For deductions about the **past**, use **must have** and **can't have** + past participle:

- Two people knocked on their friends' door; nobody answered. "They **must have gone** out." (= no other explanation)
- I can't find my scarf. I **must have left** it on the bus.
- "We lived next to the airport for years." "Really? It **must have been** deafening."
- Amir hasn't replied. He **can't have seen** my email.
- She tripped over the step. She **can't have been looking** where she was going.

Structure:

| Subject | Modal | Verb form | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| I / you / he / they … | **must have** / **can't have** | **been** + adjective/noun | He **must have been** ill. |
| | | **been -ing** | She **can't have been listening**. |
| | | past participle | They **must have forgotten**. |

You can use **couldn't have** instead of *can't have*:

- Amir **couldn't have seen** my email.
- She **couldn't have been listening**.

Common errors:

- ❌ You mustn't be tired (as a deduction). ✅ You **can't be** tired. (*mustn't* is prohibition, not deduction)
- ❌ He must forgot. ✅ He **must have forgotten**.
- ❌ It must has been expensive. ✅ It **must have been** expensive.

**See also:** can't ("I can't swim") → Unit 26; must ("I must go") → Units 31–32.

#### Question
Your neighbour's car is always parked outside and the lights are on every evening. A friend asks whether he travels a lot for work. Which reply is the correct deduction?

#### Answer
"He **can't travel** much – his car is always here." When the evidence tells us something is **not possible**, we use *can't*. *Mustn't* expresses prohibition ("he is not allowed to"), not a logical conclusion. *Must travel* would draw the opposite (illogical) conclusion, and *can't to travel* is ungrammatical.

### Question 38

#### Question
Compare: (a) "That restaurant **must be** good – there's a queue every night." (b) "That restaurant **can't be** good – it's always empty." Which explanation is correct?

#### Answer
In (a), the evidence (a nightly queue) leads the speaker to a **positive** certainty, expressed with *must*. In (b), the evidence (always empty) leads to a **negative** conclusion, expressed with *can't* – the speaker believes it is not possible that the restaurant is good. Both refer to the present, both are deductions, and *can't* here is not about ability or permission.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. (a) is an obligation; (b) is a lack of ability.
2. (a) expresses positive certainty from evidence; (b) expresses that the speaker believes something is not possible, also from evidence. (Correct)
3. (a) is about the present; (b) is about the past.
4. (b) is incorrect – the negative of *must be* is *mustn't be*.
