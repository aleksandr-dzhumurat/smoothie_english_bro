# Unit 76 — the 4 (the giraffe / the telephone / the old etc.)

# Theory

## A the + singular noun for a type (the giraffe, the telephone)

We can use **the + singular countable noun** to talk about a **type** of animal, machine, invention, currency etc. — the whole class, not one specific example:

- The cheetah is the fastest land animal. (= cheetahs as a species)
- The electric car is becoming more popular.
- Who invented the printing press?
- The yen is the currency of Japan.

Musical instruments follow the same pattern: *Can you play the cello?* / *The drums are my favourite instrument.*

Compare **a** (one example) and **the** (the type):

| a/an (one specific example) | the (the type/class) |
|---|---|
| I'd love to own a violin. | I've never learnt to play the violin. |
| We spotted a kangaroo by the road. | The kangaroo is native to Australia. |

Note: **man** (no *the*) = the human race: *the history of man*. ❌ *the man* (that would be one particular man).

## B the + adjective for groups of people (the old, the rich)

**the + adjective** (no noun) refers to a group of people who share that quality:

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| the old / the elderly | the rich / the poor | the homeless | the sick / the injured |
| the young | the unemployed | the disabled | the blind / the deaf |

- Should the rich pay more tax?
- The government promised more help for the unemployed.

These expressions are **plural** in meaning and take a plural verb: *The elderly are often lonely.* They never take *-s*: ❌ *the olds*, ❌ *the poors*.

For **one person** use adjective + noun: *a rich woman*, *a homeless man*, *an elderly person*. ❌ *a homeless*, ❌ *an unemployed*.

## C Nationality words

**the + nationality adjective** = the people of that country (plural meaning). This works with adjectives ending in **-ch / -sh**:

- the French, the Dutch, the British, the English, the Spanish, the Irish, the Welsh
- The Dutch are famous for cycling. ❌ *Dutch are famous*

These cannot be singular: ❌ *a French*, ❌ *an English*. Say *a Frenchman / a French woman*, *an English boy*.

Nationality words ending in **-ese / -ss** also take **the** for the people (*the Japanese, the Vietnamese, the Swiss*), and these **can** be singular: *a Japanese, a Swiss*.

Other nationality words have a normal **-s plural**, usually without *the*:

| one person | the people |
|---|---|
| a Brazilian | Brazilians |
| a Turk | Turks |
| a Pole | Poles |

*Brazilians love football.*

In every case you can also say **adjective + people**: *Spanish people, Chinese people, Turkish people are very hospitable.*

### Question 37

#### Question
Which sentence talks about a type of animal rather than one specific animal, and is grammatically correct?

#### Answer
The correct sentence is *The polar bear is threatened by climate change.* Here **the polar bear** means the species as a whole — a type of animal, not one particular bear. This is the "the + singular noun for a class" use. *A polar bear is threatened* would refer to one unspecified bear; *Polar bear is threatened* lacks the article a singular countable noun requires; *The polar bears are threatened* is grammatical but refers to a specific group of bears (e.g. the ones in a particular zoo), not the species.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. A polar bear is threatened by climate change.
2. The polar bear is threatened by climate change. (Correct)
3. Polar bear is threatened by climate change.
4. The polar bears in this enclosure represent the whole species.

### Question 39

#### Question
Spot the mistake: "Charities in the city do a lot for the homeless, and a new hostel was opened last year for the unemployeds and the poor."

#### Answer
The mistake is *the unemployeds*. Group expressions of the form **the + adjective** (*the homeless, the poor, the unemployed*) are plural in meaning but never take a plural **-s** ending. The correct form is *the unemployed*. *The homeless* and *the poor* are used correctly. Note also that for one person you would say *an unemployed man/person*, not *an unemployed*.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. *the homeless* should be *the homeless people*, because *homeless* is an adjective.
2. *the poor* should be *the poors*, since it is plural.
3. *the unemployeds* should be *the unemployed*: the + adjective never takes -s. (Correct)
4. *for the homeless* should be *for a homeless*.

### Question 40

#### Question
A learner writes: "Yesterday I met an English at the conference." Why is this wrong, and what is the correct version?

#### Answer
The sentence is wrong because nationality words ending in **-sh/-ch** (*English, Spanish, French, Dutch*) cannot be used as singular nouns: ❌ *an English*, ❌ *a French*. For one person use adjective + noun: *an English man / woman / person*, or the noun *an Englishman*. **The English** (with *the*) is possible only in the plural meaning "English people in general". By contrast, words ending in *-ese* or *-ss* **can** be singular (*a Japanese, a Swiss*), which is why option 4 is a false generalisation.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. It should be *I met the English*, because nationality words always take *the*.
2. It should be *I met an English person* (or *an Englishman*): -sh/-ch nationality adjectives cannot stand alone as a singular noun. (Correct)
3. It should be *I met English*, because nationality words never take an article.
4. It is wrong because no nationality word can be singular; you must always say *people*.

### Question 41

#### Question
Which sentence about the people of a country is grammatically correct?

#### Answer
The correct sentence is *Italians are proud of their coffee.* Nationality words that are not *-ch/-sh/-ese/-ss* form a normal plural with **-s** and are used **without the** when speaking generally: *Italians, Germans, Mexicans*. *The Italian are proud* is wrong because *Italian* (unlike *the French*) does not work as a plural group noun; *Italian are proud* has no plural marking; and *The Italians people* mixes a plural noun with *people*. Note that *The Italians* alone would also be acceptable in many contexts, but *Italian people* or *Italians* is the standard general form.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. The Italian are proud of their coffee.
2. Italian are proud of their coffee.
3. Italians are proud of their coffee. (Correct)
4. The Italians people are proud of their coffee.
