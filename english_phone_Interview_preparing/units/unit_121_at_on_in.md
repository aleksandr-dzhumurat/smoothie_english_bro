# Unit 121 — at/on/in (time)

# Theory

## A The three basic choices

| preposition | used for | examples |
|---|---|---|
| **at** | clock times and points of the day | at 7.30, at noon, at midnight, at dinnertime, at dawn |
| **on** | days and dates | on Tuesday, on Tuesdays, on 3 March, on Valentine's Day, on her birthday |
| **in** | longer periods: months, years, decades, centuries, seasons | in October, in 1998, in the 1960s, in the 21st century, in spring, in the future |

- The parcel came **at** 9 o'clock. It came **on** Monday. It came **in** March.

## B Fixed expressions with at

- **at the moment / at the minute / at present / at this time** = now: *Sorry, the manager is out at the moment.*
- **at the same time**: *Two phones rang at the same time.*
- **at the weekend / at weekends** (American English: *on the weekend / on weekends*): *We usually go hiking at weekends.*
- **at Christmas / at Easter** (the whole holiday period) but **on Christmas Day** (the specific day).
- **at night** = during nights generally: *Owls hunt at night.*
  **in the night** = during one particular night: *The baby cried twice in the night.*

## C Parts of the day: in vs on

| general | with a named day |
|---|---|
| in the morning(s) | on Tuesday morning(s) |
| in the afternoon(s) | on Sunday afternoon(s) |
| in the evening(s) | on Friday evening(s) |

- *I run in the mornings.* but *I run on Saturday mornings.*
- Once a day-name appears, the preposition switches to **on** (not ~~in Saturday morning~~).

## D No preposition before last / next / this / every

- *We're flying out next Thursday.* (not ~~on next Thursday~~)
- *She moved house last September.* (not ~~in last September~~)
- *He phones every Sunday.*

**On** is often dropped before days in informal English:
- *See you (on) Wednesday.* / *The shop shuts early (on) Monday afternoons.*

Note: *in April* cannot lose its preposition (❌ *He arrived April* ✅ *He arrived in April*), and *at the same time* cannot become ~~in the same time~~.

## E in + period of time = "from now" or "how long it takes"

1. Future point measured from now:
   - *The results will be published in two weeks.* (= two weeks from now)
   - *Hold on, I'll be with you in a second.*
2. Duration needed to complete something:
   - *She finished the marathon in just under four hours.* (= it took her four hours)
   - *He can solve the puzzle in thirty seconds.*

**See also:** on/in time, at/in the end → Unit 122; in/at/on (position) → Units 123–125; in/at/on (other uses) → Unit 127; American English → Appendix 7.

### Question 45

#### Question
One of these four sentences contains a mistake. Which one, and why?

#### Answer
**"We're having a barbecue on next Sunday" is wrong.** We do not use *at/on/in* before *last, next, this* or *every*: the correct sentence is *We're having a barbecue next Sunday.* The other three are fine: *on* may be dropped before a day name (*See you Tuesday*), *at weekends* is standard British English, and *at the moment* means "now".

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. "See you Tuesday" — a preposition is always needed before days.
2. "We're having a barbecue on next Sunday" — no preposition is used before *next*. (Correct)
3. "I play football at weekends" — it should be *in weekends*.
4. "She's busy at the moment" — it should be *in the moment*.

Your colleague says: "The printer is broken, but the technician will be here ___ twenty minutes. Last time he fixed it ___ five minutes." Which preposition fills both gaps, and what does each one mean?

#### Answer

#### Correct variant
