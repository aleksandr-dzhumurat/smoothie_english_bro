# Unit 137 — Phrasal verbs 1 Introduction

# Theory

## A What a phrasal verb is

A phrasal verb is a verb + a small adverb/particle such as:

| | | | | | | |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| in | on | up | away | by | about | over |
| out | off | down | back | through | along | forward / round / around |

With verbs of movement the particle usually keeps its literal, directional sense:

- **get on** — *The tram was packed, so we couldn't get on.*
- **drive off** — *He slammed the door and drove off.*
- **come back** — *Nina is flying out on Monday and coming back on Friday.*
- **turn round** — *Someone called my name, so I turned round.*

## B The particle changes the meaning

Very often the second word gives the verb a new, idiomatic meaning that you have to learn as a unit:

| Phrasal verb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| break down | stop working (a machine) | Our washing machine broke down again yesterday. |
| find out | discover | Did you ever find out who left the note? |
| take off | leave the ground (a plane) | The flight took off two hours late. |
| give up | stop trying | The puzzle was impossible, so I gave up. |
| get on | make progress, do | How did you get on in the interview? |
| get by | manage, survive | My Spanish is basic, but I get by. |

More phrasal verbs are covered in Units 138–145.

## C Phrasal verb + preposition

Some phrasal verbs are followed by a preposition, making a three-word unit:

| Phrasal verb | + preposition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| look up | at | We looked up at the fireworks. |
| run away | from | The dog ran away from the vet. |
| keep up | with | Slow down — I can't keep up with you. |
| look forward | to | I'm really looking forward to the weekend. |

## D Position of the object

Many phrasal verbs take an object. With a **noun** object there are two possible positions:

- *I switched off the heater.* **or** *I switched the heater off.*
- *Please pick up those papers.* **or** *Please pick those papers up.*

With a **pronoun** object (it / them / me / him / her / us) only one position is possible — the pronoun goes **between** the verb and the particle:

| ✅ Correct | ❌ Wrong |
|---|---|
| I switched it off. | ~~I switched off it.~~ |
| Please pick them up. | ~~Please pick up them.~~ |
| Don't wake her up. | ~~Don't wake up her.~~ |
| I'll throw it away. | ~~I'll throw away it.~~ |

Contrast with section C: in *look after her* or *keep up with them*, *after* and *with* are prepositions, so the pronoun **must** follow them.

#### Question
"These trainers are soaking wet. I'm going to ______ before I come inside." Which sentence is correct?

#### Answer
The correct sentence is "I'm going to **take them off**." When the object of a separable phrasal verb is a pronoun (*them*), it must go between the verb and the particle. "Take off them" is always wrong. Option 3 uses *off of*, which is not standard British English, and option 4 ("take them out") changes the meaning.

### Question 39

#### Question
"Sorry I missed the start of the meeting. My car ______ on the ring road and I had to wait for a mechanic." Which phrasal verb fits?

#### Answer
The correct answer is "My car **broke down**." *Break down* means to stop working (for machines and vehicles). *Break up* means to end a relationship or split into pieces, *break out* means to start suddenly (war, fire) or escape, and *break off* means to stop speaking or detach a piece. Only *break down* explains why a mechanic was needed.

#### Correct variant

4. broke down (Correct)

### Question 41

#### Question
Two sentences with *get on*: (a) "The bus stopped and we ______." (b) "How did you ______ at the dentist's?" Which statement about *get on* in (a) and (b) is true?

#### Answer
Both gaps take **got on / get on**, but the meanings differ: in (a) *get on* is literal movement — boarding the bus — while in (b) *get on* is idiomatic and means "make progress / do" (How did it go?). This illustrates section A vs section B of the unit: the same phrasal verb can keep the literal sense of the particle or take on a special meaning. The other options either claim the meanings are identical or suggest a different phrasal verb is needed.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Both mean "board a vehicle"; sentence (b) is a mistake and should be "get in".
2. (a) is literal (board the bus); (b) is idiomatic (make progress / how did it go). (Correct)
3. Both are idiomatic and mean "make progress".
4. (a) should be "get by" and (b) should be "get on"; they cannot be the same verb.
