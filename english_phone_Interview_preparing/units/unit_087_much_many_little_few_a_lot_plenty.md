# Unit 87 — much, many, little, few, a lot, plenty

# Theory

## A Which word with which noun

| quantifier | uncountable nouns | plural nouns |
|---|---|---|
| much / little | much noise, little patience | — |
| many / few | — | many students, few mistakes |
| a lot of / lots of / plenty of | a lot of noise, plenty of patience | lots of students, plenty of chairs |

**plenty of** = more than enough:
- Relax – there’s plenty of time before boarding.
- There’s plenty to see in the old town.

## B much is mainly for negatives and questions

In positive statements, especially spoken, **much** sounds unnatural; use **a lot (of)** instead:
- We didn’t spend much. **but** We spent a lot. (❌ We spent much.)
- Do you travel much? **but** I travel a lot.

However, **too much / so much / as much** are fine in positive sentences:
- I ate too much at lunch.
- She has so much energy.

**many** and **a lot of** are both fine everywhere:
- Many shops close early on Sundays. / A lot of shops close early on Sundays.
- Are there many hotels? / Are there a lot of hotels?
- There aren’t many buses after ten. / There aren’t a lot of buses after ten.

With time words we prefer **many**: *many years, many months, many days* (not usually *a lot of years*).

## C little = not much, few = not many (negative idea)

- Hugo works long shifts. He has little time to see his family. (= not much, less than he’d like)
- Aisha has just moved. She has few friends in the city yet. (= not many)

Often strengthened with **very**: *very little sleep*, *very few visitors*.

## D a little = some (a small amount), a few = some (a small number)

- We have a little time – let’s get a coffee. (enough for a coffee)
- "Do you speak Portuguese?" "A little." (enough to communicate)
- I’ve made a few friends here and we go out regularly. (not many, but enough)
- "When did you last see Omar?" "A few weeks ago." (= 3 or 4)

## E little vs a little, few vs a few

The article changes the tone from negative to positive:

- He knew **little** Japanese, so ordering food was a struggle. (almost none – negative)
- He knew **a little** Japanese, so he managed to order. (some – positive)
- She has **few** worries. (hardly any – good news)
- She has **a few** worries. (some – a slight concern)

After **only** we always use **a little / a few**:
- Quick – we only have a little time. (❌ only little)
- The hamlet had only a few cottages. (❌ only few)

### Question 45

#### Question
Which sentence expresses a POSITIVE idea (there is enough for the purpose)?

#### Answer
**"I have a little money, so I can buy us lunch."** *A little* = some, a small but sufficient amount. *Little money* means "almost no money", a negative idea, and would not lead naturally to buying lunch. *Few money* is impossible because *money* is uncountable, and *only little* is never used – it must be *only a little*.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. I have little money, so I can buy us lunch.
2. I have few money, so I can buy us lunch.
3. I have a little money, so I can buy us lunch. (Correct)
4. I have only little money, so I can buy us lunch.
