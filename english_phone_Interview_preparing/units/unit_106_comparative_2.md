# Unit 106 — Comparative 2 (much better / any better etc.)

# Theory

## A much / a lot / far / a bit / a little / slightly + comparative

You can say how big the difference is by putting one of these words before the comparative:

| big difference | small difference |
|---|---|
| much, a lot, far | a bit, a little, slightly |

- The new laptop is **much faster** than my old one. (or *a lot faster*)
- Renting is **far more expensive** here than in my home town.
- The second bedroom is **slightly smaller** than the first.
- Could you turn the music down **a bit**? — Could you play it **a little more quietly**?

❌ very better / very more expensive ✅ much better / much more expensive

## B any / no + comparative

*Any* and *no* before a comparative mean "not even a little":

- I've explained it three times; I'm not explaining it **any more clearly** than that.
- Their garden is **no bigger** than ours. (= it isn't any bigger than ours)
- Has the pain got **any better** since yesterday?
- This route is quicker and it's **no more dangerous**.

## C better and better, more and more

Repeating a comparative shows continuous change:

- Rents in the city are getting **higher and higher**.
- Her drawings are getting **better and better**.
- The queue grew **longer and longer**.
- **More and more** students are choosing to study abroad.

With *more* + adjective, repeat only *more*: *more and more expensive* (not *more expensive and more expensive*).

## D the … the …

*The + comparative … the + comparative* says that one thing depends on another:

- **The earlier** we set off, **the less** traffic we'll meet.
- **The more** you practise, **the easier** it gets.
- **The bigger** the wedding, **the higher** the bill.

Short fixed forms: *the sooner the better*, *the more the merrier*, *the cheaper the better*. — "What size box do you want?" "**The smaller the better.**"

## E older and elder

The normal comparative of *old* is **older**: *This bridge is older than the castle.* (not *elder*)

*Elder* is used only for people within a family, before a noun: *my elder brother, their elder daughter*. *Older* is also fine here: *my older brother*.

We do not use *elder* after a verb or with *than*: ❌ My brother is elder than me. ✅ My brother is older than me.

Choose the correct sentence to describe a big difference in price between two flights.

#### Answer

### Question 39

#### Question
Join these ideas using *the … the …*: "If you leave the cake in the oven longer, it gets drier." Which version is correct?

#### Answer

### Question 40

#### Question
Which sentence contains a mistake?
(a) My elder sister lives in Leeds.
(b) My sister is two years elder than me.
(c) This is the oldest building in the street.
(d) My grandfather is much older than he looks.

#### Answer
Sentence (b) is wrong. *Elder* can only be used before a noun for a family member (*my elder sister*); it cannot be used after a verb or with *than*. The comparative needed here is *older*: *My sister is two years older than me.* Sentence (a) is a correct use of *elder*, sentence (c) uses the regular superlative *oldest* correctly for a building, and sentence (d) uses *much older* correctly.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Sentence (a) — *elder* cannot be used before a noun; it should be *older sister*.
2. Sentence (c) — buildings take *eldest*, not *oldest*.
3. Sentence (b) — *elder* cannot be used with *than*; it should be *older than me*. (Correct)
4. Sentence (d) — *much* cannot go before *older*; it should be *very older*.
