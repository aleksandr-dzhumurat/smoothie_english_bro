# Unit 32 — must / mustn’t / needn’t

# Theory

## A. must and mustn't

**You must do something** = it is necessary that you do it.

- The train leaves in five minutes. We **must** run.
- Nobody else knows about the plan, and you **must** keep it that way.

**You mustn't do something** = don't do it (it is forbidden, dangerous or a bad idea).

- The baby is asleep. We **mustn't** make a sound.
- This information is confidential – you **mustn't** share it with clients.

## B. needn't and don't need to

**You needn't do something** = it is **not necessary** to do it (but you may if you want).

- The shop is open until ten, so we **needn't** rush.
- Omar has his own key. He **needn't** wait for us.

You can also say **don't/doesn't need to**:

- We **don't need to** rush.

Watch the form: *needn't* is a modal, so it is followed by the bare infinitive; *need* as an ordinary verb takes *to*:

| Modal | Ordinary verb |
|---|---|
| You **needn't** book. | You **don't need to** book. |
| ❌ *You needn't to book.* | ❌ *You don't need book.* |

**needn't vs mustn't** – the meanings are opposite in force:

- You **needn't** call Grandma tonight – I've already spoken to her. (= not necessary)
- You **mustn't** call Grandma tonight – she's having an early night. (= don't do it)

## C. needn't have (done)

Imagine a couple who queued for an hour to buy concert tickets at the box office. When they arrived, the hall was half empty and tickets were on sale at the door. They can say: *We **needn't have queued**.* This means they **did** queue, but now they know it **was not necessary**.

Compare present and past:

- Relax, the exam will be easy. You **needn't** worry. (= it isn't necessary to worry)
- The exam was easy. You **needn't have** worried. (= you did worry, but there was no need)

## D. needn't have (done) vs didn't need to (do)

| Form | What it tells us |
|---|---|
| He **needn't have** bought a ticket | He **did** buy one; it turned out to be unnecessary. |
| He **didn't need to** buy a ticket | It **wasn't necessary**; we don't know / it doesn't matter whether he bought one. |

- Why did Ella cook so much food? She **needn't have** made three courses. Two would have been plenty.
- Ella **didn't need to** cook, so she ordered a takeaway. (not necessary → she didn't)
- Ella **didn't need to** cook, but she loves cooking, so she did. (not necessary → she did anyway)

*He didn't have to buy a ticket* means the same as *he didn't need to*.

### Question 45

#### Question
Read the two sentences: (a) "Sofia didn't need to bring a laptop, but she brought one anyway." (b) "Sofia needn't have brought a laptop." What is the difference?

#### Answer
In (a), *didn't need to* states that bringing a laptop was not necessary; whether she brought one is a separate fact, given by the second clause (she did). In (b), *needn't have brought* already tells us that she **did** bring it and that we now know it was pointless. So both sentences can describe the same situation, but (b) contains the information "she did it" in the verb form itself, while (a) has to add it explicitly. The other options are wrong: neither form is limited to written English, *needn't have* is not future, and *didn't need to* does not imply she failed to bring it.

¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. (a) and (b) mean the same, but (b) is only used in formal writing.
2. (a) says it wasn't necessary and separately tells us she brought one; (b) itself implies she brought it and it turned out unnecessary. (Correct)
3. (a) means she didn't bring a laptop; (b) means she did.
