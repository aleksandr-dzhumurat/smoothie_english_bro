# Unit 39 — if I knew … I wish I knew …

# Theory

## A. if + past for an imagined present

Picture Daniel, who would like to send his old teacher a birthday card, but he doesn't have her address. He says: **I'd send her a card if I knew her address.** He doesn't know it, so he says *if I knew* – he is imagining a situation that is not real.

When we imagine a present situation like this, we use **if + past** (*if I knew / if you were / if we didn't*). The meaning is **present**, not past:

- I'd travel a lot more **if I had** a bigger budget. (but I don't have one)
- **If I didn't enjoy** my job, I'd look for another. (but I do enjoy it)
- **If you were** in charge, what would you change?
- It's a shame she can't swim. It would be handy **if she could**.

## B. wish + past

We use the **past** in the same way after **wish**. *I wish …* expresses **regret** that something is not as we would like it to be:

| reality | wish |
|---|---|
| I don't know her address. | **I wish I knew** her address. |
| I can't play the piano. | Do you ever **wish you could** play an instrument? |
| The train is packed. | **I wish there weren't** so many passengers. |
| I have to work on Sunday. | **I wish I didn't have to** work on Sunday. |

Compare *glad* and *wish*:

- I'm **glad I work** from home. (I do, and I like it)
- I **wish I worked** from home. (I don't, unfortunately)

## C. were or was

After *if* and *wish* you can use **were** instead of **was** with *I / he / she / it*. Both are correct:

- **If I were** you, I'd take the earlier flight. or **If I was** you, …
- We'd sit outside **if it weren't** so windy. or … **if it wasn't** so windy.
- **I wish Tomas were** here. or **I wish Tomas was** here.

## D. no would after if / wish

*Would ('d)* belongs in the **main clause**, not after *if* or *wish*:

- If I **lived** nearer, I **would** visit more often. ❌ *If I would live nearer*
- Who **would** you call if you **needed** help? ❌ *if you would need*
- I wish I **had** a car. ❌ *I wish I would have a car*
- I wish it **were** Friday. ❌ *I wish it would be Friday*

(*I wish … would …* exists but has a different meaning – see Unit 41.)

## E. could = would be able to / was able to

*Could* can mean either:

- **would be able to**: She **could** find a better flat (= she would be able to) …
- **was/were able to**: … if she **could** afford higher rent. (= if she were able to)
- I wish I **could** help you. (= I wish I were able to)

**See also:** could ➜ Units 26–27; if I do / if I did ➜ Unit 38; if I had known / I wish I had known ➜ Unit 40; wish ➜ Unit 41.

### Question 31

#### Question
Alicia lives in a tiny flat with no outdoor space and she loves gardening. Which sentence expresses her regret correctly?

#### Answer
"**I wish I had** a garden." After *wish*, we use the past simple to talk about a present situation we would like to be different: she doesn't have a garden and regrets this. "I wish I have" wrongly uses the present. "I wish I would have" is a common error – *would* is not used after *wish* for this meaning. "I wish I had had" is the past perfect, which would refer to a past regret ("I wish I had had a garden when the children were small"), not the present.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. I wish I have a garden.
2. I wish I would have a garden.
3. I wish I had a garden. (Correct)
4. I wish I had had a garden.

### Question 32

#### Question
Your friend is about to buy a very expensive phone he can't really afford. You want to advise him. Which sentence is correct?

#### Answer
"**If I were you, I wouldn't** buy it." *If I were you* (or *If I was you*) is the standard way to give advice by imagining yourself in the other person's position; *wouldn't* goes in the main clause. "If I would be you" wrongly puts *would* in the *if*-clause. "If I am you" uses the present for an impossible situation – the past is required. "If I were you, I didn't buy" has the wrong verb in the main clause: it needs *wouldn't buy*, not past simple.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. If I would be you, I wouldn't buy it.
2. If I were you, I wouldn't buy it. (Correct)
3. If I am you, I wouldn't buy it.
4. If I were you, I didn't buy it.

### Question 33

#### Question
Read: "I'd cycle to work if the office weren't so far away." Which statement about this sentence is true?

#### Answer
The sentence describes the **present**: the office **is** far away, so the speaker does **not** cycle to work. The past form *weren't* after *if* marks an imagined situation, not past time; *were* here is an alternative to *was* and is perfectly correct (both are acceptable after *if* with *it*). It does not say the office used to be far away, and there is no need to change *weren't* to *wasn't* – that is merely another correct option.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. The office used to be far away, so the speaker cycled in the past.
2. The sentence is wrong: *weren't* must be changed to *wasn't* because the subject is *the office*.
3. The office is far away now, so the speaker doesn't cycle; *weren't* marks an imagined present situation. (Correct)
4. The speaker cycles to work and is glad the office is nearby.

### Question 35

#### Question
Read: "Priya could get the promotion if she could work in the Berlin office." What does each *could* mean?

#### Answer
The first *could* (in the main clause, *Priya could get*) means "**would be able to**" – it is the conditional result. The second *could* (in the *if*-clause, *if she could work*) means "**was/were able to**" – it describes the imagined condition, as the past form of *can*. Both refer to the present/future situation, not the past; neither is a request or a past ability that actually existed.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Both mean "was able to" and describe something that happened in the past.
2. The first means "would be able to"; the second means "was/were able to" (the past form of *can* in the if-clause). (Correct)
3. The first is a polite request; the second means "would be able to".
4. Both are mistakes – *could* is never used in conditional sentences.
