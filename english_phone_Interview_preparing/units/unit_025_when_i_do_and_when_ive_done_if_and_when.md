# Unit 25 — when I do and when I've done / if and when

# Theory

## A. when + present (not will) for the future

Look at a sentence with two parts:

- **I'll text you when I land.**
  - main part: *I'll text you* (future with *will*)
  - time clause: *when I land* (present simple, even though the landing is in the future)

After **when**, we use the present to talk about the future – **not** *will*:

- We'll start the meeting when everyone **is** here. (not *when everyone will be*)
- When you **see** Farida, give her my regards. (not *when you will see*)
- Turn the lights off when you **leave**. (not *will leave*)

The same rule applies after **while / before / after / as soon as / until (till)**:

- What will you do while I**'m** at the conference? (not *while I will be*)
- Before you **go**, sign the form, please.
- Stay here until the rain **stops**.
- I'll ring you as soon as I **hear** anything.

## B. when I've done: present perfect in time clauses

After **when / after / until / as soon as**, you can also use the **present perfect**:

- You can have the tablet when I**'ve finished** with it.
- Don't mention the surprise while Ali is here. Wait until he**'s left**.

The present perfect stresses that the first action is **complete before** the second one begins – the two actions do not overlap:

- When I**'ve sent** the invoice, I'll go for lunch. (= first the invoice is sent, *then* lunch)

Don't use the present perfect when the two actions happen **together**:

- When I **see** Dr Lee, I'll ask about the results. (not *when I've seen* – the asking happens during the seeing)

Often either form is possible:

- I'll leave as soon as I **finish** / as soon as I**'ve finished**.
- You'll feel warmer after you **have** / after you**'ve had** a hot drink.

## C. if and when

After **if**, we also use the present for the future:

- I'll be surprised if he **turns up** on time. (not *if he will turn up*)
- If we **don't** leave now, we'll miss the bus.

Choose **if** for things that will *possibly* happen (or not), and **when** for things that are *sure* to happen:

| Uncertain → **if** | Certain → **when** |
|---|---|
| **If** it snows tonight, the school might close. | **When** the exam finishes, we're all going for a coffee. |
| Don't worry **if** I'm a bit late. | I'll pay you back **when** I get my salary. |

Compare:

- I may go to the market later. (possible) → **If** I go, I'll buy some olives.
- I'm going to the market later. (certain) → **When** I go, I'll buy some olives.

Common errors:

- ❌ I'll call you when I'll arrive. ✅ … when I **arrive**.
- ❌ When it rains tomorrow, I'll stay in. (uncertain event) ✅ **If** it rains tomorrow, …

### Question 41

#### Question
Your brother is still using the hairdryer. You want it after him. Which request best shows that his use must be finished before you take it?

#### Answer
"Can I have the hairdryer when you**'ve finished** with it?" The present perfect after *when* stresses that his finishing is **complete before** you take it; the two actions do not happen at the same time. *When you'll finish* wrongly uses *will* in a time clause. *When you finished* is past tense. *When you finish* is grammatically possible, but it does not signal the completion-before-the-other-action idea as clearly, which is what the question asks for.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants

2. Can I have the hairdryer when you finished with it?
3. Can I have the hairdryer when you've finished with it? (Correct)
4. Can I have the hairdryer when you finish with it later?

### Question 42

#### Question
Which sentence is **wrong**, and why?

#### Answer
Variant 3 is wrong: "When I've spoken to Marta, I'll ask her about the trip." Asking about the trip happens **during** the conversation with Marta, not after it, so the two actions occur together; the present perfect (which separates them) is inappropriate. The correct form is "When I **speak** to Marta, I'll ask her about the trip." Variants 1, 2 and 4 are fine: they either use the present simple in a time clause or use the present perfect for an action that is genuinely complete before the next one.

### Question 44

#### Question
Compare: (a) "I'll lock up **when** everyone has left." (b) "I'll lock up **if** everyone has left." Which explanation of the difference is correct?

#### Answer
In (a), *when* shows the speaker is **sure** everyone will leave; the sentence just states the timing of locking up. In (b), *if* shows the speaker is **not sure** everyone will have left; locking up depends on whether that condition is met. Both sentences correctly use the present perfect *has left* in the subordinate clause (no *will*). The difference lies in certainty, not in tense or formality.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. (a) is about the past; (b) is about the future.
2. (a) treats everyone leaving as certain; (b) treats it as only possible, a condition for locking up. (Correct)
3. (b) is wrong because *if* cannot be followed by the present perfect.
4. (a) is informal and (b) is formal; the meaning is the same.
