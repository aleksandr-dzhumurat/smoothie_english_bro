# Unit 10 — Present perfect continuous and simple (I have been doing and I have done)

# Theory

## A Activity vs. result

Two pictures of Rosa:

| Picture 1 | Picture 2 |
|---|---|
| Rosa has sawdust in her hair and a hammer in her hand. | The old shed is gone; a neat new one stands in its place. |
| *She has been building* a shed. | *She has built* a shed. |
| **Present perfect continuous** (*has been building*): we are thinking about the **activity**. It doesn't matter whether it is finished — here it isn't. | **Present perfect simple** (*has built*): the important thing is that the job is **complete**. We are thinking about the **result**, not the activity. |

## B More contrasts

| I have been doing (activity) | I have done (result / completion) |
|---|---|
| My clothes smell of smoke. *I've been lighting* the barbecue. | The barbecue is ready. *I've lit* it. (= finished) |
| Dan *has been spending* too much recently. He needs a budget. | Dan *has spent* all his savings. His account is empty. |
| Good to see you! *What have you been doing* since we last met? | Where's the umbrella I lent you? *What have you done* with it? |
| You're soaked. *Have you been swimming*? | *Have you ever swum* in the sea? |

## C How long vs. how much / how many

| Continuous: **how long** (still happening) | Simple: **how much / how many / how many times** (completed actions) |
|---|---|
| *How long have you been writing* that essay? | *How many pages have you written*? |
| Priya is answering emails. *She's been answering* them all afternoon. | Priya *has answered* forty emails today. |
| *They've been rehearsing* since ten o'clock. | *They've rehearsed* the song six times this week. |
| I'm learning Korean, but *I haven't been learning* it for long. | I'm learning Korean, but *I haven't learnt* many words yet. |

Duration of an ongoing activity → continuous. A quantity or a count of finished things → simple.

## D Verbs not used in the continuous

State verbs such as **know, have (= possess), like, belong** (see Unit 4A) stay in the present perfect **simple** even for duration:

- *I've known* Marta for fifteen years. (not I've been knowing)
- *How long have you had* that laptop? (not have you been having)

Exception: **want** and **mean** can appear in the continuous:

- *I've been meaning* to call my aunt, but I keep forgetting.
- *I've been wanting* to see that exhibition for months.

❌ *How long have you been knowing him?* ✅ *How long have you known him?*

#### Question
Jonas comes in from the garden with grass stains on his knees and mud on his hands. Which sentence best describes the situation?

#### Answer
"He's been planting vegetables" is correct. The evidence (stains, mud) points to a recent **activity**, and we don't know or care whether it is finished — that is the job of the present perfect continuous. "He's planted vegetables" (simple) would emphasise the completed result, e.g. a finished vegetable bed. "He plants vegetables" states a habit. "He's planting vegetables" says he is doing it at this moment, but he has come inside.

### Question 36

#### Question
You meet a woman who tells you she is a novelist. Which pair of questions is correctly formed?

#### Answer
"How many novels have you written? How long have you been writing?" is correct. Counting completed works (*how many*) takes the present perfect simple, while asking about the duration of an ongoing career (*how long*) takes the present perfect continuous. The distractors swap the forms ("have you been writing" with *how many*, "have you written" with *how long*), or use the present continuous ("are you writing"), which cannot express duration up to now.

#### Correct variant

### Question 37

#### Question
Your friend asks whether you have finished the book she lent you. In which reply is the meaning "finished, you can have it back" expressed correctly?

#### Answer
"I've read it, so here it is" is correct. The present perfect simple focuses on the **completed** action and its result — the book is finished and can be returned. "I've been reading it, so here it is" is contradictory: the continuous emphasises the activity without implying completion, so it doesn't justify handing the book back. "I've been reading it, but I haven't finished it yet" is a fine sentence but has the opposite meaning. "I'm reading it, so here it is" (present continuous) says the reading is still in progress, which again contradicts returning the book.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. I've been reading it, so here it is.
2. I've been reading it, but I haven't finished it yet.
3. I've read it, so here it is. (Correct)
4. I'm reading it, so here it is.
