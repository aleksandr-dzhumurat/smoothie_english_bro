# Unit 8 — Present perfect 2 (I have done)

# Theory

## A A period until now: life experience

Imagine two colleagues chatting at lunch:

- Omar: *Have you travelled* much, Sofia?
- Sofia: Yes, *I've been* to quite a few countries.
- Omar: *Have you ever been* to Japan?
- Sofia: Yes, *I've been* there twice. But *I haven't been* to Korea.

They are talking about Sofia's **life** — a period that started in the past and continues **until now**. For such periods we use the present perfect.

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| Have you **ever** …? | *Have you ever tried* scuba diving? |
| I have **never** … | *We've never owned* a dog. |
| I haven't … | I can't judge the book — *I haven't read* it. |
| … **twice / three times** | Sara loves that film. *She's watched* it four times. |
| the best/worst … I've **ever** … | It's the strangest meal *I've ever eaten*. |

**been (to)** = visited: *I've never been to Iceland. Have you been there?*

## B Unfinished periods: recently, so far, since, today …

The present perfect goes with any time expression describing a period that reaches up to the present:

- *Have you spoken* to Karim **recently**?
- *I've met* dozens of new people **in the last few weeks**.
- The launch is going smoothly. *There haven't been* any complaints **so far**.
- It's freezing here. *It's snowed* every day **since we arrived**.
- Lovely to see you! *We haven't caught up* **for ages**.

The same applies to **today, this morning, this week, this year** — as long as the period is **not yet over** at the time of speaking:

- *I've had* three meetings **today**. (the day isn't finished)
- *Have you taken* any time off **this year**?
- *I haven't seen* the manager **this morning**. Have you?

Timeline: ┌── past ──────────── now ┐ — the period includes the present moment.

## C It's the first time something has happened

After *It's the first / second / third time …* we use the **present perfect**, not the present simple:

Nadia is on a motorbike, looking nervous. It's her first ride. We can say:

- *It's the first time she has ridden* a motorbike. (not she rides)
- or *She hasn't ridden* a motorbike before.
- or *She has never ridden* a motorbike before.

More examples:

- Leo has spilt his drink again. *This is the second time this has happened.* (not this happens)
- Yuki is texting her brother again. *It's the fourth time she's texted him* this evening.

❌ *It's the first time I drive on the left.* ✅ *It's the first time I've driven on the left.*

### Question 28

#### Question
You want to ask a new acquaintance whether, at any point in her life, she has tried skiing. Which question is correct?

#### Answer
"Have you ever been skiing?" is correct. Asking about experience over someone's whole life — a period continuing until now — requires the present perfect, and *ever* is the natural adverb for "at any time in your life". "Did you ever go skiing?" places the experience in a finished past period, which is not the intended meaning in British English (it would suit, for example, "when you lived in Austria"). "Have you ever go skiing?" uses the base form instead of the participle. "Do you ever ski?" asks about a current habit, not past experience.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Did you ever go skiing?
2. Have you ever been skiing? (Correct)
3. Have you ever go skiing?
4. Do you ever ski?

### Question 29

#### Question
It is 4 p.m. Your colleague says: "____ five cups of coffee today — I really should stop." Which option fits?

#### Answer
"I've had" is correct. *Today* is a period that has not finished at the time of speaking (it's only 4 p.m.), so the present perfect is used to count actions within it. "I had" (past simple) would treat today as a completed period, which is only appropriate at the very end of the day or when looking back. "I have" (present simple) states a habit, not a count so far. "I've have" uses the wrong participle form.

#### Correct variant

#### Variants
1. I had
2. I have
3. I've have
4. I've had (Correct)

#### Question
Kenji is holding chopsticks awkwardly at a restaurant. His friend says: "Don't worry — ____ chopsticks." Which sentence is grammatically correct?

#### Answer
"It's the first time he's used chopsticks" is correct. "It's the first time he uses" (present simple) is a very common error but is wrong. "It's the first time he is using" (continuous) is also not used in this structure. "It's the first time he used" (past simple) is not the British English norm here.

#### Answer
