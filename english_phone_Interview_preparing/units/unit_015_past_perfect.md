# Unit 15 — Past perfect (I had done)

# Theory

## A Form and basic use

Situation: Luca and Mei both went to the same book launch. Luca left at 7.30; Mei arrived at 8. So when Mei arrived, Luca wasn't there — he **had left**.

*had left* is the **past perfect (simple)**: **had + past participle**.

| Subject | had | past participle |
|---|---|---|
| I / you / we / they (= I'd, you'd …) | had | gone / seen / finished / left … |
| he / she / it (= he'd, she'd, it'd) | had | gone / seen / finished / left … |

Negative: *hadn't gone*; question: *Had she gone?*

When we tell a story in the past, we pick a starting point in the past simple — *Mei arrived at the launch.* If we then want to mention something that happened **before** that point, we use the past perfect:

- When Mei arrived, Luca **had already left**.
- We got to the station and discovered that the last train **had gone**.
- Ines didn't want to watch the film with us. She**'d seen** it twice already.
- At first I thought I**'d found** a bargain; later I realised I**'d been** cheated.
- The couple next to us at the concert looked lost. They **hadn't been** to an opera before. / They**'d never been** to an opera before.

So the past perfect is "the past of the past": it marks the earlier of two past events.

## B Present perfect vs past perfect

The present perfect looks back from **now**; the past perfect looks back from a **point in the past**.

| Present perfect (have done) — viewed from now | Past perfect (had done) — viewed from a past moment |
|---|---|
| Who's that man? I**'ve met** him somewhere. | I couldn't place him. I**'d met** him somewhere. |
| We aren't tired. We**'ve just had** a nap. | We weren't tired. We**'d just had** a nap. |
| The fridge is empty. Nobody **has done** the shopping. | The fridge was empty. Nobody **had done** the shopping. |

Shift the whole scene into the past, and *have* becomes *had*.

## C Past simple vs past perfect

Both describe the past, but the past perfect signals that one thing happened **earlier** than the other.

| Past simple | Past perfect |
|---|---|
| "Was Dev there when you got in?" "Yes, but he **left** soon after." (he left *after* I arrived) | "Was Dev there when you got in?" "No, he**'d already left**." (he left *before* I arrived) |
| Rita wasn't in when I called. She **was** at the gym. | Rita **had just got** in when I called. She**'d been** at the gym. |

If two events simply happen one after the other in the order told, the past simple is enough: *I opened the door and walked in.* Use the past perfect only when you need to step back to an earlier time.

### Question 31

#### Question
Compare: (a) "When we arrived at the cinema, the film started." (b) "When we arrived at the cinema, the film had started." What is the difference?

#### Answer
In (a) the film started **after** we arrived — we were on time. In (b) the film **had started** before we arrived — we were late. The past simple presents the two events in sequence, while the past perfect marks the film starting as the earlier of the two. Both sentences are grammatical; they simply describe different situations. Variants claiming one is ungrammatical, or that they mean the same, are wrong.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. They mean the same; the past perfect just sounds more formal.
2. In (a) we were on time and the film began after we got there; in (b) the film was already running when we got there. (Correct)
3. Sentence (b) is ungrammatical because two past-perfect events cannot be joined with "when".
4. In (a) the film began before we arrived; in (b) it began after we arrived.
