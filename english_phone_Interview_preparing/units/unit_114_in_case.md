# Unit 114 — in case

# Theory

## A in case = because it is possible that …

**In case something happens** means "because it is possible that it will happen". You do something **now** as a precaution against something that **might** happen later:

- Take a jacket **in case** it gets cold this evening. (= because it's possible it will get cold)
- I'll save a copy of the file **in case** the laptop crashes.
- Let's leave early **in case** the roads are busy.
- I'll text her the address **in case** she's forgotten it. (= because it's possible she has forgotten)

**Just in case** suggests a smaller possibility: *I'm sure the shop takes cards, but I'll bring some cash just in case.* (*Just in case* can stand alone at the end of a sentence.)

After **in case** we use a **present tense** for the future, not *will*:

- ✅ Write the time down **in case** you **forget**. ❌ *in case you will forget*

## B in case vs if

They are **not** the same.

| in case | if |
|---|---|
| explains **why** you do something **now**, as a precaution | describes what you will do **later, only if** the condition is true |
| *I'll pack a snack in case the train is delayed.* (I pack it now, whether or not it's delayed) | *I'll buy a snack if the train is delayed.* (only if it's delayed will I buy one) |
| *Here's my spare key in case you're locked out.* | *Ring me if you're locked out.* |
| *Insure the camera in case it gets damaged.* (do it before) | *Claim on the insurance if it gets damaged.* (do it after) |

The *in case* action happens **before** and **regardless** of the possible event; the *if* action happens **after** and **depends on** it.

## C in case + past

To explain why somebody did something in the past, use **in case** + **past tense**:

- I left the porch light on **in case** they **came** back late.
- She took a spare battery **in case** the first one **ran out**.
- We knocked a second time **in case** they **hadn't heard** us.

## D in case of

**In case of** + noun = "if there is …". It is typical of notices and instructions and has a different pattern from *in case* + clause:

- **In case of** fire, do not use the lift. (= if there is a fire)
- **In case of** a power cut, the generator starts automatically.

Do not confuse: *in case of rain* (notice-style, = if it rains) vs *in case it rains* (precaution).

## Extra practice

### Question 26

#### Question
Leo is going hiking. You are worried he might get thirsty. Which sentence gives your advice correctly?

#### Answer
"Take a big bottle of water **in case you get thirsty**." *In case* introduces the possible future problem and explains why he should act now; after *in case* we use the present simple for the future, not *will*. "In case you will get thirsty" is wrong because *will* is not used after *in case*. "If you get thirsty" changes the meaning – it would mean take the water only at the moment you become thirsty, which does not make sense as advice before leaving. "In case of you get thirsty" mixes *in case of* (+ noun) with a clause.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Take a big bottle of water in case you will get thirsty.
2. Take a big bottle of water in case you get thirsty. (Correct)
3. Take a big bottle of water if you get thirsty.
4. Take a big bottle of water in case of you get thirsty.

### Question 27

#### Question
Which sentence means: "I am giving you the number now, as a precaution. Whether or not you need it, you'll have it"?

#### Answer
"I'll give you the emergency number **in case** you need it." *In case* describes something you do now because of a possibility later – the number is given regardless of whether the need arises. "I'll give you the number if you need it" means you will give it later, and only if the need appears, so the person does not have it now. "When you need it" also postpones the giving until the need arises. "Unless you need it" gives the opposite, nonsensical meaning.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. I'll give you the emergency number if you need it.
2. I'll give you the emergency number when you need it.
3. I'll give you the emergency number in case you need it. (Correct)
4. I'll give you the emergency number unless you need it.

### Question 28

#### Question
Yesterday Hana emailed a report to her manager. Then she also printed a copy, because she thought the email might not arrive. Which sentence correctly explains what she did?

#### Answer
"She printed a copy **in case the email didn't arrive**." When we explain why somebody did something in the past, we use *in case* + past tense (*didn't arrive*). "In case the email doesn't arrive" mixes past action with present-tense possibility – possible in some contexts, but not the natural report of a finished past situation; here the book's pattern is past + past. "In case the email wouldn't arrive" and "in case the email will not arrive" are wrong because *would* / *will* are not used after *in case*.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. She printed a copy in case the email will not arrive.
2. She printed a copy in case the email wouldn't arrive.
3. She printed a copy in case the email didn't arrive. (Correct)

### Question 29

#### Question
A notice in a hotel corridor reads: "____ fire, use the stairs, not the lift." Which words complete it correctly?

#### Answer
"**In case of** fire, use the stairs, not the lift." *In case of* + noun means "if there is …" and is the standard form on notices and instructions. "In case fire" is wrong because *in case* (without *of*) needs a clause with a subject and verb. "In case there will be fire" wrongly uses *will* and is not notice style. "If in case of fire" doubles up two structures.

#### Correct variant

1. In case fire
2. In case of (Correct)

### Question 30

#### Question
One of these sentences uses *in case* wrongly. Which one, and what should it be?

#### Answer
Sentence 4 is wrong: "Report the theft to the police in case your phone is stolen." The correct word is *if*: *Report the theft to the police if your phone is stolen.* The other sentences are correct uses of *in case*: they describe precautions taken beforehand (a lock, a copy, an umbrella) against something that might happen.
