# Unit 111 — still / any more / yet / already

# Theory

## A still

**Still** says that a situation or action is continuing – nothing has changed or stopped:

- It's midnight and Marta is still at her desk.
- When I called, they were still having dinner.
- Do you still play the piano, or did you give it up?

**Still** can also mean "in spite of this": *She earns a fortune, but she's still not satisfied.*

Position: **still** normally goes in the middle of the sentence, next to the verb (see Unit 110).

## B not … any more / not … any longer / no longer

These say that a situation **has changed** – it was true before, but not now.

| form | position | example |
|---|---|---|
| not … any more / anymore | end of sentence | Tom doesn't live in Leeds any more. |
| not … any longer | end of sentence | Tom doesn't live in Leeds any longer. |
| no longer | middle (with the verb) | Tom no longer lives in Leeds. |

- **Any more** can be written as one word (**anymore**) or two.
- **No longer** is positive in form and goes before the main verb; do not use *no more* in this meaning: ✅ *We're no longer neighbours.* ❌ *We're no more neighbours.*

Contrast **still** and **not … any more**: *Nadia still works in the café, but Omar doesn't work there any more.*

## C yet

**Yet** = "until now". It is used mainly in **negatives** and **questions**, and it shows that the speaker **expects** something to happen. It normally goes at the **end**:

- The parcel hasn't arrived yet.
- Have you chosen a name for the baby yet?
- "When are you moving?" "We haven't decided yet."

**Yet** is very common with the present perfect (see Unit 7C).

Compare **yet** and **still**:

- Ravi moved here a year ago and is **still** looking for a flat. (the search continues)
- Ravi moved here a year ago and **hasn't found** a flat **yet**. (expected result hasn't happened)
- Is the baby **still** crying? / Has the baby **stopped** crying **yet**?

**Still** is also possible in a negative sentence, *before* the negative verb, and it sounds stronger – surprise or impatience:

- I emailed the landlord last week. He **hasn't answered yet**. (neutral: I expect an answer soon)
- I emailed the landlord a month ago and he **still hasn't answered**. (he should have by now!)

## D already

**Already** says something happened **sooner than expected**:

- "Is the meeting starting soon?" "It's already started."
- Shall I explain the rules, or do you already know them?
- I only bought this phone in May and it's already broken.

Position: middle of the sentence (*She has already gone*) or end (*She has gone already*).

## Quick summary

| word | idea | typical position |
|---|---|---|
| still | continuing, unchanged | middle |
| not … any more / any longer | changed, finished | end |
| no longer | changed, finished | middle |
| yet | until now (negatives/questions), expected | end |
| already | sooner than expected | middle or end |

**See also:** Present perfect + already/yet ➜ Unit 7C; Word order ➜ Unit 110

### Question 36

#### Question
Five years ago Daniel worked in a bank and played football every weekend. Today he works in a school, but he plays football every weekend as before. Which pair of sentences describes Daniel now correctly?

#### Answer
"He doesn't work in a bank any more. He still plays football every weekend." The bank job is a situation that has changed, so we need *not … any more* (or *not … any longer* / *no longer works*). Football is a situation that is continuing unchanged, so we use *still* in the middle of the sentence. *Yet* is wrong because it is used for something expected but not yet happened, and *already* would suggest the football started sooner than expected, which is not the idea here.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. He doesn't work in a bank yet. He already plays football every weekend.
2. He doesn't work in a bank any more. He still plays football every weekend. (Correct)
3. He still doesn't work in a bank. He plays football every weekend any more.
4. He no more works in a bank. He plays football every weekend still yet.

### Question 37

#### Question
Your colleague says: "Sophie used to be a member of the running club, but she left in March." Which sentence with *no longer* expresses the same idea in the correct position?

#### Answer
"Sophie is no longer a member of the running club." *No longer* goes in the middle of the sentence, between the verb *be* (or the auxiliary) and the rest, and the sentence stays positive in form. *Any more* and *any longer* need a negative verb and go at the end (*Sophie isn't a member any more*), so "Sophie is a member no longer any more" mixes the two patterns. *No more* is not used for this meaning in modern English.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Sophie is no more a member of the running club.
2. Sophie isn't no longer a member of the running club.
3. Sophie is a member of the running club no longer any more.
4. Sophie is no longer a member of the running club. (Correct)

### Question 38

#### Question
It is 9.15. The lecture was supposed to start at 9.00, but the lecturer hasn't appeared. A student who *expects* him to arrive soon says: "The lecturer isn't here ____." Which word fits, and why not the others?

#### Answer
"The lecturer isn't here **yet**." *Yet* is used in negative sentences (and questions) to mean "until now" and it signals that the speaker expects the thing to happen. It goes at the end of the sentence. *Still* in a negative would go before the verb ("he still isn't here") and would express stronger impatience; at the end of the sentence it is wrong. *Already* means "sooner than expected" and does not fit a negative statement here, and *any more* would mean the lecturer used to be there but has stopped being there.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

1. still
2. already
3. yet (Correct)

### Question 39

#### Question
Which sentence expresses the strongest feeling of impatience about a reply that should have come long ago?

#### Answer
"I wrote to the council two months ago and they **still haven't replied**." *Still* + negative (still hasn't / still haven't) shows that the speaker thinks the thing should have happened before now – a feeling of surprise or annoyance. "They haven't replied yet" is neutral: it simply says the reply hasn't come but is expected. "They haven't replied any more" is wrong because *any more* means a situation has stopped, and "they already haven't replied" is not a possible combination.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. I wrote to the council two months ago and they haven't replied yet.
2. I wrote to the council two months ago and they still haven't replied. (Correct)
3. I wrote to the council two months ago and they haven't replied any more.
4. I wrote to the council two months ago and they already haven't replied.

### Question 40

#### Question
One of these sentences contains a mistake. Which one, and what is wrong?

#### Answer
Sentence 3 is wrong: "I used to enjoy jazz, but I don't listen to it still." When we say a situation has changed or stopped, we use *not … any more* / *not … any longer* at the end, or *no longer* in the middle: *I don't listen to it any more.* *Still* is for something that continues, and in a negative it goes before the verb (*I still don't listen*), not at the end. The other three sentences are correct: *already* is fine in the middle (1), *yet* at the end of a question (2), and *still* in mid-position (4).

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. "Shall I book the tickets?" "No need – I've already booked them."
2. Has the shop opened yet?
3. I used to enjoy jazz, but I don't listen to it still. (Correct)
4. It's been three hours and the printer is still not working.
