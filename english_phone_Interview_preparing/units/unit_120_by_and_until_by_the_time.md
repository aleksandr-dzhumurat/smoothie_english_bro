# Unit 120 — by and until / by the time …

# Theory

## A by … = not later than

**By** + a time means "at that time or before it – not later than":

- The application must be submitted **by Friday**. (= on Friday or earlier, not after Friday)
- We need to leave the house **by 7.15** to catch the train. (= at 7.15 at the latest)
- The parcel was posted on Monday, so it should be here **by Thursday**.
- Where's the taxi? It should be here **by now**. (= now or before now – it's late)

Typical uses: deadlines, expiry dates (*Use by 3 May*), arrival times.

## B until (till) = how long something continues

**Until** (or informal **till**) says that a situation **continues up to** a certain time and then stops:

- Let's wait **until** the rain eases off.
- The library is open **until 9 pm** on weekdays.
- I didn't get home **until** midnight. (= I got home at midnight, not before)

### until vs by

| until – something **continues** up to a time | by – something **happens** not later than a time |
|---|---|
| Dana will be away **until** Thursday. (she comes back on Thursday) | Dana will be back **by** Thursday. (Thursday at the latest) |
| I'll be in a meeting **until** 3.30. (the meeting ends at 3.30) | I'll have finished the meeting **by** 3.30. (not later than 3.30) |
| Wait here **until** I come back. | I'll be back **by** six. |

A useful test: *until* goes with verbs describing a **state or continuing activity** (*stay, wait, be away, work*); *by* goes with verbs describing a **completed action or point** (*arrive, finish, be back, decide, pay*).

## C by the time …

**By the time** + clause = "at the moment when this happens, the other thing will already be done / have happened". It stresses that the first thing takes a long time.

**Future:** *by the time* + present tense, main clause with *will* / *will have done*:

- **By the time we reach** the station, the train **will have left**. (the train leaves between now and our arrival)
- **By the time you finish** all these forms, the office **will be** closed.

**Past:** *by the time* + past simple, main clause usually past perfect (or a past state):

- **By the time she got** to the theatre, the play **had already started**.
- I **was** completely soaked **by the time I found** shelter.
- It took ages to find a parking space. **By the time we sat down**, the first course **had been served**.

**By then** / **by that time** replace a full *by the time* clause when the moment is already clear:

- Tom finally arrived at eleven, but **by then** everyone **had gone** home.
- The results come out in June; **by that time** I'**ll have started** my new job.

**See also:** will be doing and will have done ➜ Unit 24; by (other uses) ➜ Units 42B, 60B, 128

### Question 32

#### Question
Your manager says: "I need the report on my desk not later than Wednesday." Which sentence expresses this with *by* or *until* correctly?

#### Answer
"I need the report on my desk **by Wednesday**." *By* + a time means "not later than" – the report can arrive on Wednesday or before, which is exactly what "not later than Wednesday" means. *Until Wednesday* would mean the report should stay on the desk continuously up to Wednesday, which is not the idea. *By the time Wednesday* is incomplete (*by the time* needs a clause), and *till Wednesday* has the same continuation meaning as *until*.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. I need the report on my desk until Wednesday.
2. I need the report on my desk by Wednesday. (Correct)
3. I need the report on my desk by the time Wednesday.
4. I need the report on my desk till Wednesday.

### Question 33

#### Question
Complete the pair: "Julia is on a course this week. She'll be away ____ Friday, so she'll be back ____ Saturday." Which words fill the gaps?

#### Answer
"She'll be away **until** Friday, so she'll be back **by** Saturday." *Be away* is a continuing state, so it goes with *until* – the state lasts up to Friday and then ends. *Be back* is a point event that must happen not later than a time, so it takes *by*. Reversing them (option 1) gives the wrong meaning: "away by Friday" would mean she will have left not later than Friday, and "back until Saturday" would mean she stays back only up to Saturday.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. by / until
2. until / until
3. until / by (Correct)
4. by / by

### Question 34

#### Question
It's 8.05 and the bakery closes at 8.15. It takes you fifteen minutes to walk there. Which sentence describes the situation correctly with *by the time*?

#### Answer
"**By the time I get** there, the bakery **will have closed**." For the future, *by the time* is followed by a present tense (*get*), and the main clause uses *will have* + past participle to show that the closing will be complete before the arrival. Option 1 uses *will get* after *by the time*, which is wrong (present tense is required). Option 3 uses the past perfect *had closed*, which belongs to past narration, not this future situation. Option 4 uses *until* + clause, which describes continuation rather than a completed event.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. By the time I will get there, the bakery will have closed.
2. By the time I get there, the bakery will have closed. (Correct)
3. By the time I get there, the bakery had closed.
4. Until I get there, the bakery will have closed.
