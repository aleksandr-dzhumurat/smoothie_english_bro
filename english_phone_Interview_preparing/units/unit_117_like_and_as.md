# Unit 117 — like and as

# Theory

## A like = similar to / the same as

**Like** is a **preposition**, so it is followed by a **noun**, a **pronoun** or an **-ing** form:

| like + | example |
|---|---|
| noun | Their garden is huge – it's **like a park**. (not *as a park*) |
| pronoun | I can't stand traffic **like this**. (not *as this*) |
| -ing | Reading his handwriting is **like solving a puzzle**. (not *as solving*) |

Other typical uses: *It smells like fresh bread. He looks like his grandfather. It sounds like someone knocking.*

**Like** can also mean **for example**; **such as** is an alternative:

- She plays several instruments, **like** the cello and the flute. / … **such as** the cello …

## B as + subject + verb

**As** = in the same way as / in the same condition as. It is normally followed by a **clause** (subject + verb):

- Please leave the kitchen **as you found it**.
- Do it **as the manual explains**.

Informally, **like** is also used before a clause: *Leave the kitchen like you found it.* But before a pronoun alone only *like* works: *Do it like this.* (not *as this*)

Fixed expressions with **as**:

- **as usual** – *The bus was late, as usual.*
- **as always** – *As always, Sami forgot his keys.*
- **the same as** – *My laptop is the same as yours.* (not *the same like*)

## C as you know / as I said etc.

**As** + subject + verb often refers to something already known, expected or said:

- **As you know**, the office is closed on Friday. (= you already know this)
- **As I said** in my email, the deadline is Tuesday.
- She got the promotion, **as everyone expected**.
- **Do as you're told.** / **They did as they had agreed.** (*as* = what)

**Like** is not usual in these expressions, except in informal **like I said**.

## D as = in the position / role of

**As** can also be a preposition (**as** + noun), but then it means "in the role of / in the form of" – a fact, not a comparison:

| as (it is really so) | like (similar, but not really so) |
|---|---|
| Hana works **as a nurse**. (that is her job) | Hana looked after us **like a nurse**. (she isn't a nurse) |
| He used the box **as a table**. (in the function of a table) | The box looked **like a table**. |

More examples: *Years ago I worked as a translator. The word "run" can be used as a noun. Bristol is great as a place to study. Her resignation came as a surprise.*

## Quick check

- similar to → **like** + noun/pronoun/-ing
- in the same way as → **as** + clause (informally *like* + clause)
- role, job, function → **as** + noun
- fixed: *as usual, as always, the same as, as you know, as I said*

### Question 1

#### Question
**Exercise 117.1 (item 1)** — In some of these sentences you need *like* (not *as*). Correct the sentence where necessary, or say it is OK.
It's raining again. I hate weather as this.

#### Answer
**I hate weather like this.** — *like* is a preposition meaning 'similar to / such as' and is followed by a noun or pronoun: *weather like this*. *As* + noun would mean 'in the role of', which is nonsense here.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. I hate weather like this. (Correct)
2. The sentence is correct (OK)
3. I hate weather such this.
4. I hate weather as if this.

### Question 43

#### Question
Compare: (a) "During the festival, Tomás worked **as** a security guard." (b) "During the festival, Tomás worked **like** a security guard." Which explanation is correct?

#### Answer
In (a) *as a security guard* means that was his actual job – *as* + noun = in the role of. In (b) *like a security guard* means he wasn't a security guard, but he behaved the way one does – *like* = similar to. So the two words give clearly different meanings here. Option 1 wrongly treats them as interchangeable, option 3 reverses the meanings, and option 4 is false because *like* + noun is perfectly correct.

¯\_(ツ)_/¯

1. The two sentences mean the same; *like* is just more informal than *as*.
