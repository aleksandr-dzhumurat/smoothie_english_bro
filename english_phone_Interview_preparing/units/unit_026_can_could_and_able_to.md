# Unit 26 — can, could and (be) able to

# Theory

## A. can

*Can* + infinitive (without *to*) says that something is **possible**, **allowed**, or that someone has the **ability** to do it:

- From the top floor you **can see** the whole harbour. (possible)
- "I've forgotten my charger." "You **can borrow** mine." (allowed / offer)
- **Can** your daughter **swim** yet? (ability)
- I **can meet** you after work if that suits you.
- The word "run" **can be** a verb or a noun.

The negative is **can't** (= cannot):

- Sorry, I **can't make** it to the meeting on Monday.

## B. (be) able to

*Be able to* means the same as *can*, but *can* is more usual:

- We **are able to see** the harbour from the top floor. (possible, but *can see* is more natural)

*Can* has only **two forms**: *can* (present) and *could* (past). It has no infinitive, no *-ing* form, no perfect. Whenever you need those, use **be able to**:

| can | be able to (when *can* is impossible) |
|---|---|
| I **can't** concentrate today. | I **haven't been able to** concentrate all week. |
| Sara **can** come on Friday. | Sara **might be able to** come on Friday. |
| Ravi **can** read Arabic. | Candidates **must be able to** read Arabic. |
| — | I'd love **to be able to** play the cello. |

## C. could = past of can

*Could* is often the past of *can*. It is especially common with verbs of perception and mental state: **see, hear, smell, taste, feel, remember, understand**:

- Our tent was by the river – we **could hear** the water all night.
- The moment I opened the fridge I **could smell** something rotten.
- I sat at the back and **couldn't see** the screen properly.

*Could* also describes general **past ability** or past **permission**:

- My aunt **could play** three instruments.
- As students we **could use** the library 24 hours a day. (= we were allowed to)

## D. could vs was able to / managed to

Use **could** for **general** ability in the past and with *see/hear* etc.

But when someone **succeeded in doing something on one specific occasion**, use **was/were able to** or **managed to**, **not** *could*:

- The road was flooded, but the driver **was able to** find another route. (not *could find*)
- I lost my keys, but I **managed to** get into the flat through a window. (not *could get*)

Compare:

- Elena was a brilliant chess player as a child. She **could beat** her teachers. (general ability)
- Elena played her cousin yesterday. He's strong, but she **managed to beat** him. (a specific success)

The **negative couldn't** is fine in **all** situations – general or specific:

- My grandmother **couldn't** drive.
- I searched everywhere but **couldn't** find my passport.
- He played well but **couldn't** beat her.

**See also:** could (do) and could have (done) → Unit 27; must and can't → Unit 28; can/could you …? → Unit 37.

### Question 37

#### Question
Compare: (a) "When he was young, Marco could run a mile in under five minutes." (b) "Marco was exhausted, but he managed to finish the race." Which explanation is correct?

#### Answer

(b) describes **one specific occasion** on which Marco **succeeded**, so *managed to* (or *was able to*) is needed and *could* would be wrong. Both sentences are correct as written; the contrast is between general ability and a single achievement, not between formality or certainty.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯
