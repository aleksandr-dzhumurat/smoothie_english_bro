# Unit 108 — Superlative (the longest / the most enjoyable etc.)

# Theory

## A Form: -est or most …

A superlative compares one thing with all the others in a group:

- Which is **the tallest** building in the city?
- What's **the most useful** app on your phone?

The rules mirror the comparative (Unit 105): *-est* for short words, *most* for longer ones.

| base | comparative | superlative |
|---|---|---|
| cold | colder | the coldest |
| big | bigger | the biggest |
| busy | busier | the busiest |
| quiet | quieter / more quiet | the quietest / the most quiet |
| careful | more careful | the most careful |
| expensive | more expensive | the most expensive |

Irregular forms:

| base | superlative |
|---|---|
| good | the best |
| bad | the worst |
| far | the furthest / the farthest |

❌ the most cheap ✅ the cheapest
❌ the interestingest ✅ the most interesting

## B the / my / your + superlative

A superlative is normally preceded by *the* or a possessive (*my, Anna's*, etc.):

- Friday was **the busiest** day of the week.
- The library is **one of the oldest** buildings in town.
- Rafael is **the most reliable** colleague I've worked with.
- What's **your earliest** memory?

Superlative vs comparative:

- This café is **the cheapest** in the area. (compared with all the others)
- This café is **cheaper than** the one opposite. (compared with one other)
- Lucía is **the most patient** teacher in the school. / Lucía is **more patient than** me.

## C oldest and eldest

The regular superlative is **oldest**: *the oldest tree in the park* (not *eldest*).

*Eldest* is used only for people in a family (*their eldest son*), and *oldest* is also possible there: *Are you the eldest / the oldest of the three sisters?*

## D in or of after a superlative?

- **in** + place: *the highest mountain in Europe* (not *of Europe*); *the best room in the hotel*
- **in** + group or organisation: *the youngest player in the team*; *the newest employee in the company*
- **of** + period of time: *the coldest night of the year*; *the happiest day of my life*

## E Superlative + present perfect

A superlative is often followed by the present perfect, usually with *ever*:

- This is **the spiciest curry I've ever eaten**.
- That was **the most useful course I've done** in years.

**See also:** Comparative ➜ Units 105–107; elder ➜ Unit 106E.

### Question 40

#### Question
A colleague says, "That was the most stressful week of the year, but at least it was less stressful than my first week here." Which statement about this sentence is right?

#### Answer
Both parts are correct, and the sentence deliberately contrasts a superlative with a comparative. *The most stressful week of the year* uses a superlative because the week is compared with all the other weeks of the year; *of* is the right preposition for a period of time. *Less stressful than my first week* is a comparative because only two weeks are compared. Superlatives and comparatives can appear in the same sentence when they compare different things.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. It should be *the most stressful week in the year*; *of* is not used after a superlative.
2. Both parts are correct: a superlative for the comparison with all weeks of the year, a comparative for the comparison with one other week. (Correct)
3. The sentence is wrong because a superlative and a comparative cannot appear in the same sentence.
4. It should be *the stressfulest week*; two-syllable adjectives always take *-est*.

### Question 41

#### Question
Spot the mistake: "My grandmother's house is the eldest building in the village, and she is the eldest of six children."

#### Answer
The mistake is *the eldest building*. *Eldest* is used only for people in a family; for things (and for people who are not family members) the superlative of *old* is *oldest*: *the oldest building in the village*. The second use, *the eldest of six children*, is correct because it refers to the position of a person within a family — *the oldest* would also be fine there.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. *the eldest of six children* is wrong; people always take *oldest*, never *eldest*.
2. *the eldest building* should be *the oldest building*; *eldest* is only for family members. (Correct)
3. Both uses are wrong; *eldest* does not exist in modern English.
4. *in the village* should be *of the village* after a superlative.
