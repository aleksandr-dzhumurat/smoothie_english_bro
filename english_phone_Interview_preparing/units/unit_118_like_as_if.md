# Unit 118 — like / as if

# Theory

## A look / sound / feel + like / as if + clause

To say how somebody or something **looks, sounds or feels**, we use **like** or **as if** followed by a **subject + verb**:

- That bridge **looks like** it's about to collapse. / … **looks as if** it's about to collapse.
- Dad **sounded like** he'd been crying. / … **sounded as if** he'd been crying.
- I've slept for nine hours, but I **don't feel like** I've slept at all. / … **as if** I've slept at all.

**As though** means the same as **as if**: *I don't feel as though I've slept at all.*

Compare the two structures after *look*:

| look + adjective | look like / as if + subject + verb |
|---|---|
| You look **exhausted**. | You look **like you've been up all night**. |
| | You look **as if you've been up all night**. |

## B it looks like … / it sounds like …

The impersonal pattern **it looks like / it looks as if** and **it sounds like / it sounds as if** describes a general impression:

- The bus is twenty minutes late. **It looks like** it's been cancelled. / **It looks as if** it's been cancelled.
- **It looked like** the shop was closing, so we hurried in. / **It looked as if** …
- There's music and laughter upstairs. **It sounds like** the neighbours are celebrating. / **It sounds as though** …

## C like / as if / as though with other verbs

We also use these words after other verbs to describe **the manner** in which something is done:

- She sprinted **like** she was being chased.
- The teacher carried on **as if** the fire alarm hadn't gone off.
- When I suggested a pay rise, my boss looked at me **as though** I'd asked for the moon.

## D as if + past for an unreal present

After **as if** (and **as though**) we can use a **past tense** to talk about the **present** when the idea is **not real**:

- Tariq talks **as if he knew** everything. (he doesn't know everything)
- She orders me around **as if I were** her servant. (I'm not her servant)
- Why does he spend money **as if he was** a millionaire? (he isn't)

This is the same "unreal past" as in *if I knew …* and *I wish I knew …* (see Unit 39). The past form signals that the situation is imaginary; the time is now.

- **Was** or **were** are both possible after *I / he / she / it*: *as if he were / as if he was*.
- **Like** is not normally used with this unreal past: prefer ✅ *He acts as if he owned the place* to ❌ *He acts like he owned the place*.

Contrast real vs unreal:

- He looks as if he **is** ill. (he probably is ill – real possibility)
- He behaves as if he **were** ill. (he isn't ill; he's pretending)

**See also:** if I was/were ➜ Unit 39C; look/sound etc. + adjective ➜ Unit 99C; like and as ➜ Unit 117

### Question 23

#### Question
Your brother comes in soaked from head to toe. Which sentence is grammatically correct?

#### Answer
"You look **like you've fallen** in the river." After *look*, a clause (subject + verb) must be introduced by *like*, *as if* or *as though*. "You look like fallen" has no subject, "You look as you've fallen" uses *as* instead of *as if* (in this meaning *as* + clause would mean "in the way that", which does not fit), and "You look as if fallen" also omits the subject.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. You look like you've fallen in the river. (Correct)
2. You look like fallen in the river.
3. You look as you've fallen in the river.
4. You look as if fallen in the river.

### Question 25

#### Question
Which sentence uses the "unreal past" after *as if* correctly to describe a present situation that is not true?

#### Answer
"My little cousin talks about football **as if he were** the national coach." The past form *were* (or *was*) after *as if* shows that the idea is imaginary: he is not the coach. Sentence 1 uses the present *is*, which suggests a real possibility rather than an obviously unreal idea. Sentence 3 uses *like* with the unreal past, which the book says is not normal. Sentence 4 uses *would be*, which is not the pattern after *as if*.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. My little cousin talks about football as if he is the national coach.
2. My little cousin talks about football as if he were the national coach. (Correct)
3. My little cousin talks about football like he were the national coach.
4. My little cousin talks about football as if he would be the national coach.

### Question 26

#### Question
Compare: (a) "Rania looks **as if she's got** a fever." (b) "Rania behaves **as if she had** a fever." Which explanation is correct?

#### Answer
(a) uses a present tense after *as if*: the speaker thinks it is really possible that she has a fever – a real impression. (b) uses a past tense for the present: the speaker means she does not actually have a fever but acts that way – an unreal or pretended situation. Option 1 wrongly claims (b) is about the past; option 3 reverses the two; option 4 is false because both sentences are grammatical.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. In (b) *had* means she had a fever in the past; (a) is about now.
2. In (a) the fever is a real possibility (present tense); in (b) the past tense shows the fever is not real – she is only acting that way. (Correct)
3. In (a) the fever is imaginary; in (b) it is a real possibility.
4. Only (a) is grammatical; a past tense cannot follow *as if* when talking about the present.
