# Unit 68 — -ing clauses (He hurt his knee playing football.)

# Theory

## A Two things at the same time

Imagine Diego is on the balcony. He is watering the plants. You can join these into one sentence with an **-ing clause**:

- Diego is on the balcony **watering the plants**.

Use -ing when two actions happen **at the same time**:
- The toddler ran across the room **laughing**. (= she ran and she was laughing)
- Don’t just sit there **complaining** – help me!
- Take care **using** that knife.

Also use -ing when one action happens **during** another (the -ing action is the longer background one):
- Lena sprained her ankle **running** for the bus. (= while she was running)
- I burnt my hand **taking** the tray out of the oven.

You can add **while** or **when** before the -ing form:
- Lena sprained her ankle **while running** for the bus.
- Take care **when using** that knife.

## B One action before another: Having done …

When the first action is finished before the second begins, use **having + past participle** for the first:
- **Having paid** the bill, we walked back to the hotel.
- **Having checked** the map, she chose the coastal road.

**After -ing** has the same meaning:
- **After paying** the bill, we walked back to the hotel.

These forms belong mainly to **written** English. When the sentence starts with *Having …* or *After …*, put a **comma** after the -ing clause:
- *Having checked the map**,** she chose the coastal road.*

## C -ing to give a reason

An -ing clause at the beginning of a sentence can explain **why** something happens:
- **Feeling unwell**, Sam left the party early. (= because he felt unwell)
- **Being new to the city**, I got lost several times. (= because I was new)
- **Not wanting** to wake the children, we whispered. (= because we didn’t want to)

Negative: **not + -ing** (*Not having a ticket, he couldn’t get in.*)

For a reason that was **complete before** the main event, use **having done**:
- **Having lived** in Lisbon for years, she knew every back street. (= because she had lived there)
- **Having missed** breakfast, I was starving by eleven.

Again, this is more common in writing, and the -ing clause is followed by a **comma**:
- *Not knowing anyone at the conference**,** I sat at the back.*

### Question 26

#### Question
Combine the two sentences using an -ing clause: "Marek was standing at the bus stop. He was scrolling through his phone."

#### Answer
"Marek was standing at the bus stop **scrolling through his phone**." When two actions happen at the same time, the second is expressed as an -ing clause attached to the first. *And scrolled* is a plain past simple and does not use the -ing structure. *To scroll* would wrongly suggest purpose (he stood there in order to scroll). *Scrolled through his phone* after the main clause with no conjunction is ungrammatical.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Marek was standing at the bus stop to scroll through his phone.
2. Marek was standing at the bus stop scrolled through his phone.
3. Marek was standing at the bus stop scrolling through his phone. (Correct)
4. Marek was standing at the bus stop and scrolled his phone.

### Question 27

#### Question
Rina cut her finger. This happened while she was chopping onions. Which sentence expresses this correctly?

#### Answer
"Rina cut her finger **chopping** onions." When one action happens during another longer action, the background action is expressed with -ing (the meaning is *while she was chopping*). *Chopped onions* has no link to the main clause. *To chop* wrongly makes it a purpose. *While chopped* combines *while* with a past participle, which is not a valid clause – *while chopping* would be correct.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Rina cut her finger chopped onions.
2. Rina cut her finger chopping onions. (Correct)
3. Rina cut her finger to chop onions.
4. Rina cut her finger while chopped onions.

### Question 28

#### Question
"We finished the meeting. Then we went out for lunch." Which sentence combines these correctly to show that the first action was complete before the second?

#### Answer
"**Having finished** the meeting, we went out for lunch." *Having + past participle* signals that the first action was completed before the second. *Finishing the meeting, we went out* would mean the two happened at the same time, which is illogical. *Having finish* is wrong because *having* must be followed by a past participle. *After finished* is wrong because *after* needs an -ing form (*After finishing*).

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Finishing the meeting, we went out for lunch.
2. Having finish the meeting, we went out for lunch.
3. Having finished the meeting, we went out for lunch. (Correct)
4. After finished the meeting, we went out for lunch.

### Question 29

#### Question
Which sentence is correctly formed to mean "Because she didn’t know the way, Alina asked a passer-by"?

#### Answer
"**Not knowing** the way, Alina asked a passer-by." A negative -ing clause is formed with *not* before the -ing form. *Knowing not the way* puts *not* in the wrong position. *Don’t knowing* mixes an auxiliary with an -ing form, which is impossible. *No knowing the way* uses *no* instead of *not*; *no* modifies nouns, not verb forms.

#### Correct variant

### Question 30

#### Question
Which of the following sentences contains a mistake, and what is it?

A. Being a doctor, Hana is used to working long hours.
B. Having seen the play twice already I decided to stay at home.
C. Feeling cold, we lit the fire.
D. He walked out of the room slamming the door.

#### Answer
Sentence **B** has a punctuation mistake: when a sentence begins with *Having …*, a comma must follow the -ing clause: "Having seen the play twice already**,** I decided to stay at home." The form *Having seen* itself is correct because seeing the play was complete before the decision. Sentence A correctly uses *Being* to give a reason, C correctly uses *Feeling* with a comma, and D correctly attaches a simultaneous -ing clause at the end, where no comma is needed.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Sentence A – *Being a doctor* should be *Having been a doctor*.
2. Sentence B – a comma is missing after *already*. (Correct)
3. Sentence C – *Feeling cold* should be *Having felt cold*.
4. Sentence D – a comma is required before *slamming*.
