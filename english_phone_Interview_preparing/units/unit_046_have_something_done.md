# Unit 46 — have something done

# Theory

## A — Meaning: arranging for somebody else to do something

*Have something done* means you **arrange for another person** to do a job for you — you don't do it yourself.

- Marta fixed the leaking tap. (she did it herself)
- Marta **had the leaking tap fixed**. (a plumber did it; she arranged it)

Compare:
- A: Did you paint the fence yourself? B: No, I **had it painted** — I hate painting.
- We're **having a new kitchen fitted** next month.

## B — Word order: have + object + past participle

| have | object | past participle |
|---|---|---|
| Marta had | the tap | fixed. |
| Where do you have | your car | serviced? |
| I'm going to have | my eyes | tested. |
| He hates having | his photo | taken. |

The object comes **between** *have* and the participle. ❌ *I had cut my hair.* (this means something else — see below) ✅ *I had my hair cut.*

- How often do you have your teeth checked? (not *have checked your teeth*)
- They're having an extension built. (not *having built an extension*)

*Have* here behaves like a normal verb: questions and negatives use *do/did*: *Did you have the brakes checked?*

## C — get something done

In informal English *get* can replace *have* with the same meaning:

- You should **get** that tooth **looked at**.
- When are we going to **get** the windows **cleaned**?

## D — A second meaning: something happens to you

*Have something done* can also mean simply that **something (often unpleasant) happened to you or your belongings** — nobody arranged it:

- Ismail **had his passport stolen** at the station. (= his passport was stolen)
- She **had her arm broken** in a rugby match. (= her arm was broken)
- We **had our flight cancelled** twice last year.

Context makes the meaning clear: nobody arranges to have their passport stolen.

### Question 27

#### Question
Your neighbour's roof was leaking, so she called a roofer, who came and repaired it yesterday. Which sentence describes this correctly?

#### Answer
"She **had the roof repaired** yesterday." The structure *have + object + past participle* means she arranged for someone else to do the job. "She repaired the roof" would mean she did it herself. "She had repaired the roof" is the past perfect and again means she did it herself, at an earlier time. "She had the roof repair" lacks the past participle.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. She repaired the roof yesterday.
2. She had repaired the roof yesterday.
3. She had the roof repaired yesterday. (Correct)
4. She had the roof repair yesterday.

### Question 28

#### Question
Put the words in the correct order: (serviced / how often / your boiler / do / have / you / ?)

#### Answer
"How often **do you have your boiler serviced**?" *Have something done* takes the order *have + object (your boiler) + past participle (serviced)*, and since *have* is an ordinary verb here, the question uses *do*. "Do you have serviced your boiler" wrongly puts the participle before the object. "Have you your boiler serviced" treats *have* like an auxiliary and omits *do*. "Do you have your boiler service" uses the base form instead of the participle.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. How often do you have serviced your boiler?
2. How often have you your boiler serviced?
3. How often do you have your boiler service?
4. How often do you have your boiler serviced? (Correct)

#### Question
Which sentence uses *have something done* with the meaning "something happened to me / my belongings" rather than "I arranged for it"?

### Question 30

#### Question
Spot the mistake. A student wrote: "My laptop is slow, so tomorrow I'm going to have repaired it."

#### Answer
The correct sentence is "…I'm going to **have it repaired**." The object must go between *have* and the past participle: *have + object + past participle*. "Have repaired it" puts the participle first and reads as a present perfect (*I have repaired it*), which would mean the speaker did the repair — the opposite of what is intended. Changing the participle to *repair* or *repairing* also breaks the structure.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. It should be *have it repaired*: the object goes between *have* and the past participle. (Correct)
2. It should be *have repaired it* — the sentence is already correct.
3. It should be *have it repairing* — a continuous form is needed after *have*.
4. It should be *have to repair it* — *have* must be followed by *to*.

### Question 31

#### Question
A friend says: "You should ___ before you go on holiday — it's much more secure." Which option correctly uses the informal alternative to *have*?

#### Answer
"You should **get** the locks **changed**." In informal English *get something done* means the same as *have something done*: arrange for someone to do it. "Get changed the locks" has the wrong word order. "Get the locks change" lacks the past participle. "Get to change the locks" means you should manage/obtain the chance to change them yourself, which changes the meaning.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. get changed the locks
2. get the locks changed (Correct)
3. get the locks change
4. get to change the locks
