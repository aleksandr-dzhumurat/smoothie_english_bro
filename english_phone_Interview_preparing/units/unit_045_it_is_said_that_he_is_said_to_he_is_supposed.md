# Unit 45 — it is said that … he is said to … he is supposed to …

# Theory

## A — Two ways of reporting what people say or believe

When we report a general opinion, rumour or piece of news without saying whose it is, we can use two equivalent passive patterns:

| Pattern 1: *It is said that* + clause | Pattern 2: subject + *is said to* + infinitive |
|---|---|
| It is said that the old mill is haunted. | The old mill is said to be haunted. |
| It is believed that the painting is a forgery. | The painting is believed to be a forgery. |
| It is expected that prices will fall. | Prices are expected to fall. |

Both mean "people say / believe / expect …". The verbs most often used this way are:

**alleged, believed, considered, expected, known, reported, said, thought, understood**

When the reported event is in the **past**, pattern 2 uses the perfect infinitive **to have done**:

- It is alleged that the manager took bribes. → The manager is alleged **to have taken** bribes.
- It is reported that the ship sank in minutes. → The ship is reported **to have sunk** in minutes.
- It is thought that the passengers were rescued. → The passengers are thought **to have been rescued**.

*There* can also be the subject: *There is said to be gold under these hills.*

These structures are typical of **news reports**: *Two hikers are reported to be missing.*

## B — supposed to … (1): what people say

*Supposed to* can work like *said to*:

- That new Thai place is supposed to be excellent. (= people say it is)
- The house is supposed to have belonged to a pirate. (= people say it did)

## C — supposed to … (2): what is intended, arranged or expected

*Supposed to* also describes a **plan, duty or arrangement** — often one that isn't matching reality:

- The train is supposed to leave at 9.05, but it's already 9.20.
- You're supposed to be revising, not watching videos.
- Hana was supposed to bring the tickets, but she forgot them.
- I'm supposed to be at the dentist's in ten minutes — I'd better run.

***Not* supposed to** = not allowed or not advisable:

- You're not supposed to feed the animals.
- He's not supposed to lift anything heavy after the operation.

Note the form: *be + supposed to* (with **-d**). ❌ *He suppose to be here.* ✅ *He is supposed to be here.*

### Question 28

#### Question
A news report begins: "It is believed that the suspect left the country last night." Rewrite this beginning with *The suspect …*. Which version is correct?

#### Answer
"The suspect **is believed to have left** the country last night." When the reported event happened **before** the time of the report (last night), the personal pattern uses the perfect infinitive *to have done*. "Is believed to leave" would suggest a habitual or future action, not a completed past one. "Is believed that he left" mixes the two patterns — *that* only follows *it is believed*. "Was believed to leave" shifts the believing itself into the past and still lacks the perfect infinitive.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. The suspect is believed to leave the country last night.
2. The suspect is believed that he left the country last night.
3. The suspect is believed to have left the country last night. (Correct)
4. The suspect was believed to leave the country last night.

#### Question
People say that the company's new phone is very fast. Which sentence expresses this with *said*?

#### Answer
"The new phone **is said to be** very fast." The present state (being fast) needs the simple infinitive *to be* after *is said*. "Is said being" uses an -ing form where an infinitive is required. "Is said to have been" is the perfect infinitive and would suggest the phone *was* fast in the past.

#### Correct variant

#### Variants
1. The new phone is said that it is very fast.
2. The new phone is said to be very fast. (Correct)
3. The new phone is said being very fast.
4. The new phone is said to have been very fast.

### Question 32

#### Question
Spot the mistake. A student wrote: "Visitors are not suppose to touch the exhibits, but the guard is expected that he lets children touch them."

#### Answer
There are two errors. First, the form is *supposed* with *-d*: "Visitors are not **supposed to** touch the exhibits." Second, *expected* with a personal subject takes an infinitive, not a *that*-clause: "the guard **is expected to let** children touch them." A *that*-clause is only possible with *it* as subject (*It is expected that the guard lets …*). The option that fixes only one of the errors is therefore incomplete.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Only *suppose* is wrong; *is expected that he lets* is a normal passive report.
2. Only *is expected that* is wrong; *are not suppose to* is acceptable in informal English.
3. Both parts are wrong: it should be *are not supposed to touch* and *is expected to let*. (Correct)
4. Nothing is wrong; both structures are standard.
