# Unit 62 — Verb + preposition + -ing (succeed in -ing / insist on -ing etc.)

# Theory

## A Verb + preposition + -ing

Many verbs are followed by a fixed preposition and then an object:

| verb | preposition | object |
|---|---|---|
| We argued | about | the bill. |
| She apologised | for | the delay. |

If the object is another verb, that verb takes the **-ing** form, because a preposition cannot be followed by an infinitive:

| verb | preposition | -ing |
|---|---|---|
| We argued | about | splitting the bill. |
| She apologised | for | arriving late. |

Common combinations to learn as units:

| verb + preposition | example |
|---|---|
| approve **of** | My parents don’t approve of me staying out late. |
| decide **against** | We decided against renting a car. |
| dream **of** | I wouldn’t dream of interrupting her. |
| feel **like** | Do you feel like ordering pizza tonight? |
| insist **on** | Grandad insists on carrying his own suitcase. |
| look forward **to** | I’m looking forward to seeing the results. |
| succeed **in** | Have they succeeded in fixing the leak? |
| think **of/about** | We’re thinking of adopting a dog. |

Note that **look forward to** ends in a preposition, so it is followed by -ing (❌ *looking forward to see you*, ✅ *looking forward to seeing you*).

You can also put the person who does the action before the -ing form: *I don’t approve of **children using** phones at the table.* / *We’re looking forward to **Aunt Rosa arriving** on Friday.*

## B Verb + object + preposition + -ing

Some verbs take an object first, then the preposition and -ing:

| verb | object | preposition | -ing |
|---|---|---|---|
| They accused | the driver | of | speeding. |
| We congratulated | Femi | on | passing his driving test. |
| The snow prevented | us | from | leaving the village. |
| Nothing will stop | her | (from) | trying again. |
| The police suspected | him | of | forging the signature. |
| I thanked | the nurse | for | looking after me. |

Points to remember:
- With **stop**, *from* is optional: *You can’t stop me **going** / stop me **from going**.*
- A negative uses **not** before the -ing form: *He accused me of **not listening**.*
- Several of these verbs are common in the **passive**: *She was accused of cheating.* / *Two men were suspected of planning the robbery.*
- **Apologise** takes *to* before the person and *for* before the action: *I apologised **to** my neighbours **for** making a noise.* (❌ *I apologised my neighbours*)

### Question 26

#### Question
Complete the sentence: "Even though we offered to pay, our hosts insisted ______ for the taxi themselves."

#### Answer
"… insisted **on paying** for the taxi themselves." *Insist* is always followed by the preposition *on*, and after a preposition the verb must be in the -ing form. *Insisted to pay* wrongly treats *insist* as a verb that takes an infinitive. *Insisted paying* leaves out the required preposition, and *insisted on to pay* puts an infinitive after a preposition, which is impossible.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. on paying (Correct)
2. to pay
3. paying
4. on to pay

### Question 27

#### Question
A friend writes: "I can’t wait for the concert next week. I’m really looking forward to see the band live." What is wrong with the sentence?

#### Answer
It should be "I’m really looking forward **to seeing** the band live." In *look forward to*, the word *to* is a preposition, not part of an infinitive, so it must be followed by -ing. Learners often see *to* and automatically add a bare infinitive. The verb *look forward* itself is correct and doesn’t need changing, and *see* is a suitable verb here, so the only error is the form after *to*.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. *Looking forward* should be *looking forwards*; the rest is fine.
2. *To see* should be *to seeing*, because *to* here is a preposition. (Correct)
3. *To see* should be *for seeing*, because *look forward* takes *for*.
4. Nothing is wrong – both *to see* and *to seeing* are acceptable.

### Question 28

#### Question
Choose the correct sentence describing what happened at a border checkpoint.

#### Answer
"The officers **suspected the traveller of carrying** undeclared goods." *Suspect* takes the pattern *suspect somebody of doing something*. The other options use the wrong preposition (*for*, *to*) or the wrong form (*to carry*). Confusing the prepositions of these verb + object combinations is a typical error: *accuse … of*, *suspect … of*, *congratulate … on*, *prevent … from*, *thank … for*.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. The officers suspected the traveller for carrying undeclared goods.
2. The officers suspected the traveller to carry undeclared goods.
3. The officers suspected the traveller of carrying undeclared goods. (Correct)
4. The officers suspected the traveller of to carry undeclared goods.

### Question 29

#### Question
Which sentence correctly reports this apology: Maria said to her colleagues, "I’m sorry I didn’t finish the report on time."

#### Answer
"Maria **apologised to her colleagues for not finishing** the report on time." Two things are tested: *apologise* needs *to* before the person (❌ *apologised her colleagues*) and *for* before the action; and the negative is formed by putting *not* directly before the -ing form (*for not finishing*), not by using *didn’t* or *no*.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Maria apologised her colleagues for not finishing the report on time.
2. Maria apologised to her colleagues for didn’t finish the report on time.
3. Maria apologised to her colleagues for no finishing the report on time.
4. Maria apologised to her colleagues for not finishing the report on time. (Correct)

### Question 30

#### Question
Complete the sentence with the most natural form: "Heavy fog prevented the ferry ______ on time, so we spent the night in the port."

#### Answer
"… prevented the ferry **from leaving** on time." The pattern is *prevent somebody/something from doing something*. Unlike *stop*, which allows *stop the ferry leaving* or *stop the ferry from leaving*, *prevent* normally keeps *from*. *To leave* is impossible after *prevent* + object, and *of leaving* uses a preposition that belongs to other verbs (*accuse of*, *suspect of*).

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. to leave
2. from leaving (Correct)
3. of leaving
4. for leaving
