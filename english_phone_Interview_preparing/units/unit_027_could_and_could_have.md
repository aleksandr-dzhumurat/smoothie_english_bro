# Unit 27 — could (do) and could have (done)

# Theory

## A. could for suggestions (now / future)

*Could* is sometimes the past of *can*:

- Shh – I **can** hear footsteps. (now)
- I stopped and listened. I **could** hear footsteps. (past)

But *could* is not always past. We also use it for **possible actions now or in the future**, especially to make **suggestions**:

- "What shall we cook tonight?" "We **could** make a curry."
- "When you're in Rome, you **could** stay with my cousin." "Yes, I suppose I **could**."

*Can* is also possible here (*We can make a curry*), but **could is less definite** than *can*.

## B. could for unrealistic actions

Use **could** (not *can*) for actions that are **not realistic** or are exaggerations:

- I'm so hungry I **could** eat a horse. (not *I can eat*)

Compare three degrees:

| Sentence | Meaning |
|---|---|
| I **can** stay with my cousin in Rome. | realistic, definite |
| Maybe I **could** stay with my cousin in Rome. | possible, but less sure |
| This view is incredible. I **could** sit here for ever. | unrealistic |

## C. could for possibility now / in the future

*Could* (not *can*) says that something **is possible** now or in the future:

- That rumour **could** be true, though I doubt it. (not *can be true*)
- I don't know when the parcel is coming. It **could** arrive any minute.

Compare *can* (general truth) and *could* (a specific situation):

- Mountain weather **can** turn nasty very fast. (in general)
- It's sunny now, but it **could** turn nasty later. (this specific situation)

## D. could have (done) – the past

To talk about the **past**, use **could have + past participle**:

- I'm so tired I **could sleep** for a week. (now)
- I was so tired I **could have slept** for a week. (past)
- Things are bad, but they **could be** worse. (now)
- Things were bad, but they **could have been** worse. (past)

*Something could have happened* = it **was possible but did not happen**:

- Why did you take a taxi? You **could have come** with us.
- Mia was lucky. She **could have broken** her arm when she fell off the bike, but she's fine.

## E. couldn't and couldn't have

*I couldn't do something* = it **would not be possible**:

- I **couldn't work** night shifts. I'd never see my family. (= it wouldn't be possible for me)
- Everything's going well. Things **couldn't be** better.

For the past: **couldn't have …** (= would not have been possible):

- The wedding was perfect. It **couldn't have gone** better.

Note that **I couldn't do something** has **two meanings**:

1. it would not be possible **now** → I **couldn't** cycle 100 km today – I'm not fit enough. (= I would not be able)
2. I **was not able** (past) → I **couldn't** cycle yesterday because my bike had a flat tyre.

Common errors:

- ❌ It can be true, but I don't think so. ✅ It **could** be true …
- ❌ You could stay with me last night. (past, didn't happen) ✅ You **could have stayed** with me.

**See also:** can and could → Unit 26; couldn't have (done) → Unit 28B; could and might → Unit 29C; could I/you …? → Unit 37; could with if → Units 38C, 39E, 40D.

### Question 36

#### Question
Compare: (a) "Traffic in this city **can** be terrible." (b) "Traffic **could** be terrible tonight because of the match." Which explanation is correct?

#### Answer
(a) uses *can* for a **general** truth – traffic is sometimes terrible as a general characteristic of the city. (b) uses *could* for a **specific** possibility on one occasion (tonight). Both are correct; swapping them changes the meaning. Neither sentence is more polite than the other, and (b) is not about the past.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. (a) describes a general characteristic; (b) describes a possibility in one specific situation. (Correct)
2. (a) is about the present; (b) is about the past.
3. (b) is wrong – *could* cannot be used about the future.
4. (a) is informal; (b) is the formal equivalent with the same meaning.

### Question 37

#### Question
Read: "I couldn't go swimming this morning." Which two situations could this sentence describe, according to the unit?

#### Answer
*I couldn't do something* has two possible meanings: (1) it would not be possible now / I would not be able (e.g. "I couldn't go swimming this morning – I'm far too tired", hypothetically), or (2) I was not able to in the past (e.g. "I couldn't go swimming this morning because the pool was closed"). Which meaning applies depends on context. It never means "I was not allowed to but did it anyway", and it is not a suggestion.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Either a polite suggestion to someone else, or a past refusal.
2. Either "it would not be possible for me" (hypothetical), or "I was not able to" (past fact). (Correct)
3. Only "I was not allowed to"; *couldn't* never refers to ability.
4. Only a hypothetical meaning; for the past you must say *couldn't have gone*.
