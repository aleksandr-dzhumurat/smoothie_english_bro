# Unit 19 — Present tenses (I am doing / I do) for the future

# Theory

## A Present continuous (I am doing) for arrangements

Look at Amara's calendar for next week: *Monday — dentist, 9.30; Wednesday — dinner with Yusuf; Saturday — flying to Rome.*

- She **is going** to the dentist on Monday.
- She **is having** dinner with Yusuf on Wednesday.
- She **is flying** to Rome on Saturday.

In each case she has **already decided and arranged** it. *I'm doing something (tomorrow / next week)* = it's fixed in my plans.

- a: What **are you doing** on Friday night? ❌ (not *What do you do*)
- b: I**'m going** to a comedy show. ❌ (not *I go*)
- What time **is** Petra **arriving**? Half past six — we**'re picking** her **up** at the airport.
- I**'m not working** on Monday, so we could go somewhere.
- Dan **isn't playing** in the match on Sunday. He's pulled a muscle.

We do **not** normally use *will* for arrangements we have already made:

- What **are you doing** at the weekend? ❌ (not *What will you do*)
- Leo and Kim **are getting married** in June. ❌ (not *will get married*)

The present continuous is also used for an action that is about to start, especially with verbs of movement (*go, come, leave*):

- I'm exhausted. I**'m going** to bed. ❌ (not *I go to bed*)
- "Hurry up!" "OK, I**'m coming**." ❌ (not *I come*)

## B Present simple (I do) for timetables and programmes

For **timetables, schedules and programmes** — trains, flights, films, courses, meetings — we use the present simple:

- The last bus **leaves** at 11.40.
- What time **does** the match **start** tonight?
- The workshop **is** at ten tomorrow.

You can use the present simple for people too, when their plans are fixed like a timetable:

- I **start** my new job on the 1st.
- What time **do** you **finish** tomorrow?

But for most **personal arrangements** the continuous is more natural:

- What time **are you meeting** Petra? ❌ (not *do you meet*)

| Present continuous (personal arrangement) | Present simple (timetable) |
|---|---|
| What time **are you arriving**? | What time **does the coach arrive**? |
| I**'m going** to the theatre tonight. | The play **starts** at 7.30. |

For appointments, lessons, exams and similar, *I have / I've got* is also common:

- I **have** a driving test on Thursday. / I**'ve got** a driving test on Thursday.

### Question 36

#### Question
You want to ask a friend about their plans for Saturday evening. Which question is correct?

#### Answer
"What **are you doing** on Saturday evening?" Asking about somebody's arrangements uses the present continuous with future meaning. "What do you do" is a present-simple question about habits or jobs, not about a specific future arrangement. "What will you do" is not normally used for plans already made, and the present continuous is the natural form. "What are you going to do" is grammatical, but it asks about intentions rather than arrangements and is not the form this unit focuses on for fixed plans; the book's model for arrangements is the present continuous.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. What do you do on Saturday evening?
2. What are you doing on Saturday evening? (Correct)
3. What will you do on Saturday evening?
4. What do you doing on Saturday evening?

### Question 40

#### Question
Ivo has hurt his leg and cannot play in Saturday's match. His teammate is explaining this. Which sentence fits best?

#### Answer
"Ivo **isn't playing** on Saturday. He's hurt his leg." A change to a fixed arrangement about a person is expressed with the present continuous (negative). "Ivo doesn't play on Saturday" sounds like a general habit ("he never plays on Saturdays"), not this specific match. "Ivo won't play" is grammatically possible but not the normal way to talk about a settled arrangement, and the unit explicitly prefers the present continuous. "Ivo isn't play" is ungrammatical because the continuous needs *-ing*.

#### Correct variant

2. Ivo isn't play on Saturday. He's hurt his leg.
3. Ivo won't to play on Saturday. He's hurt his leg.
