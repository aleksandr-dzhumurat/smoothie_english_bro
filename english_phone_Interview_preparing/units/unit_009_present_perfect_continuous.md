# Unit 9 — Present perfect continuous (I have been doing)

# Theory

## A An activity that has recently stopped

You walk into the kitchen: the oven is warm, there is flour on the worktop and a smell of fresh bread. Nobody is baking at this moment, but clearly *somebody has been baking*.

The present perfect continuous is **have/has been + -ing**:

| Subject | have/has | been | -ing form |
|---|---|---|---|
| I / we / you / they | have (I've …) | been | running |
| he / she / it | has (he's …) | been | studying |

Negative: *haven't / hasn't been + -ing*. Question: *Have you been waiting? What has she been doing?*

We use it for an activity that has **just stopped or recently stopped** and whose effects are visible or relevant now:

- Your eyes are red. *Have you been crying*?
- Nadia is exhausted. *She's been revising* all evening.
- Why are your shoes so muddy? *What have you been doing*?
- *I've been speaking* to the landlord and he's agreed to fix the boiler.
- There you are! *I've been trying* to call you.

## B An activity continuing up to now: how long / for / since

Suppose the wind started blowing at noon and it is still blowing at three. *How long has it been blowing? It's been blowing for three hours.*

We use the present perfect continuous with **how long**, **for …** and **since …** for an activity that started in the past and is **still happening** (or has only just stopped):

- *How long have you been living* in Bristol? (= you still live there)
- Tomas is at his desk. *He's been working* on that report since eight o'clock.
- *We've been waiting* for the bus for twenty-five minutes.
- Grandma *hasn't been sleeping* well lately.

The activity can also be **repeated** rather than one continuous action:

- Lucía is an excellent violinist. *She's been playing* since she was six.
- Every Friday they play chess in the park. *They've been doing* that for years.

## C I am doing vs. I have been doing

| Present continuous (I am doing) | Present perfect continuous (I have been doing) |
|---|---|
| Focus: **now**, in progress at this moment | Focus: the period **up to now**, or an activity that has just stopped |
| Don't interrupt — *I'm studying*. | *I've been studying* for three hours. Time for a break. |
| Take a coat. *It's snowing*. | The path is white. *It's been snowing*. |
| Come on! *We're waiting*. | *We've been waiting* since half past two. |

❌ *I'm learning Spanish for two years.* ✅ *I've been learning Spanish for two years.*

### Question 24

#### Question
Your flatmate walks in, out of breath and sweating. Which question is the natural one to ask?

#### Answer
"Have you been running?" is correct. The present perfect continuous is used for an activity that has just stopped and whose results (being out of breath) are visible now. "Are you running?" (present continuous) asks about right now, but he is standing in the room. "Did you run?" (past simple) treats the run as a finished event with no link to his present state. "Have you run?" (present perfect simple) focuses on completion or result rather than the recent activity.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

2. Have you been running? (Correct)

#### Question

#### Answer
"has been working" is correct. The situation started in 2019 and continues now ("she still enjoys it"), and the sentence uses *since*, so the present perfect continuous is needed. "was working" (past continuous) would imply the job has ended.

### Question 26

#### Question
Which sentence is wrong, and why?

#### Answer
"I'm waiting for the plumber for two hours" is wrong: to say how long an action has been going on up to now, we need the present perfect continuous — "I've been waiting for the plumber for two hours." The present continuous describes only what is happening at this moment and cannot carry a *for*-duration. The other three sentences correctly use the present perfect continuous for duration ("has been raining since dawn"), for a repeated activity ("have been going to that café for years"), and for a recently finished activity with a present result ("You've been working hard").

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. It has been raining since dawn.
2. I'm waiting for the plumber for two hours. (Correct)
3. They have been going to that café for years.
4. You look tired — you've been working hard.

#### Question
A friend tells you that she designs websites for a living. You want to know how long she has done this. Which question is correct?

#### Answer
"How long have you been doing that?" is correct. *How long* asks about duration up to now, and the present perfect continuous is the standard form for an ongoing activity. "How long are you doing that?" wrongly uses the present continuous with *how long*. "How long do you do that?" uses the present simple, which does not express duration up to the present. "How long have you doing that?" is missing *been*.

2. How long do you do that?

### Question 28

#### Question
Choose the correct pair: (a) "Hurry up — everyone ____ for you in the car." (b) "Where were you? We ____ for you for half an hour!"

#### Answer
"(a) is waiting (b) have been waiting" is correct. In (a) the focus is on what is happening right now — people are in the car waiting at this moment — so the present continuous fits. In (b) the speaker looks back over a period up to now ("for half an hour"), so the present perfect continuous is needed. Reversing them makes (a) sound like a complaint about duration and leaves (b) unable to express *for half an hour*.
