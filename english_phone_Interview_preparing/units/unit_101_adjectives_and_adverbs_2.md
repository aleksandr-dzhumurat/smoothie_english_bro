# Unit 101 — Adjectives and adverbs 2 (well, fast, late, hard/hardly)

# Theory

## A good and well

*Good* is an adjective; its adverb is *well*. Use *good* to describe a noun and *well* to describe how something is done:

| adjective (good) | adverb (well) |
|---|---|
| Marta is a good cook. | Marta cooks well. |
| Your Spanish is good. | You speak Spanish well. |

❌ She sings very good. ✅ She sings very well.

*Well* (not *good*) is also used with past participles to make compound adjectives: *well-known, well-paid, well-dressed, well-organised, well-behaved, well-informed*.

- Our new manager is very well-organised.
- The dog is well-behaved, so it can stay inside.

*Well* can also be an adjective meaning "healthy": *"How's your grandmother?" "She's very well, thank you."*

## B fast, hard and late

These three words have the same form as adjective and adverb — there is no *-ly* adverb with the same meaning:

| adjective | adverb |
|---|---|
| Nadia is a fast swimmer. | Nadia swims fast. |
| Learning Chinese is hard. | Omar studies hard. (not studies hardly) |
| The train was late. | The train arrived late. |

Note: *lately* exists, but it means "recently", not "late": *I haven't been to the gym lately.*

## C hardly

*Hardly* is not the adverb of *hard*. It means "very little" or "almost not":

- The room was dark and I could hardly see the door. (= it was almost impossible to see)
- We've only spoken twice, so I hardly know him.

Compare the two words:

- She trained hard for the race. (= with a lot of effort)
- She hardly trained for the race. (= she trained very little)

*Hardly* goes before the main verb, not at the end: *I hardly slept.* (not *I slept hardly*). With *can/could* it means "almost impossible": *My hands were so cold I could hardly hold the pen.*

## D hardly any / hardly ever / hardly = certainly not

*Hardly* combines with *any, anybody/anyone, anything, anywhere*:

- "Is there any milk left?" "Hardly any." (= almost none)
- Hardly anybody came to the meeting. (= very few people)
- The twins look so alike that there's hardly any difference between them.

You can say *She said hardly anything* or *She hardly said anything* — both are fine.

*hardly ever* = almost never: *I hardly ever eat meat these days.*

*Hardly* also means "certainly not" when it comments on a statement:

- You've been driving for ten hours — it's hardly surprising you're exhausted.
- A missed bus is annoying, but it's hardly a disaster.

### Question 41

#### Question
A colleague writes in a report: "The presentation went good, and everyone said the speaker explained the data very well." Which part of the sentence contains a mistake, and what is the correct form?

#### Answer
The mistake is *went good*; it should be *went well*. *Good* is an adjective and can only describe a noun (*a good presentation*). After a verb like *go* (in the sense of "happen"), we need the adverb *well* to say how it happened. The second half, *explained the data very well*, is already correct because *well* is modifying the verb *explained*.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. *explained the data very well* is wrong — after *explained* the adjective *good* is needed: *explained the data very good*.
2. *went good* is wrong — after *went* we need the adverb *well*: *The presentation went well*. (Correct)
3. Both parts are wrong — it should be *went well* and *explained the data very good*.
4. There is no mistake; *good* and *well* are interchangeable after verbs in British English.

### Question 45

#### Question
Read the two sentences and decide what they mean:
(a) "Daniel tried hard to fix the printer."
(b) "Daniel hardly tried to fix the printer."
Which explanation is right?

#### Answer
Sentence (a) means Daniel made a lot of effort; *hard* is an adverb meaning "with a lot of effort". Sentence (b) means Daniel made very little effort, almost none; *hardly* means "very little / almost not". So the two sentences have almost opposite meanings. *Hardly* is not a more formal or emphatic version of *hard* — it is a different word with a different meaning, and confusing them changes what you are saying.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Both sentences mean the same; *hardly* is just the more formal adverb form of *hard*.
