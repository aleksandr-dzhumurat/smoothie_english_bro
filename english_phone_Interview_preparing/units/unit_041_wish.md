# Unit 41 — wish

# Theory

## A — wish somebody something vs. hope

You can *wish somebody* a thing: luck, success, a good journey, a happy birthday.

- We wished Daniel a safe flight.
- I wish you every happiness in your new home.

But you cannot use *wish* to say you want a future event to happen. For that, use **hope**:

- ❌ I wish the weather is nice tomorrow. ✅ I hope the weather is nice tomorrow.
- ❌ I wish you pass the exam. ✅ I hope you pass the exam.

Compare: *I wish you a pleasant flight* (noun object) / *I hope you have a pleasant flight* (clause).

## B — wish for regret about the present and the past

*Wish* also expresses regret — a situation is not the way we would like it. The verb after *wish* goes **one step back in time**:

| Regret about … | Form after wish | Example |
|---|---|---|
| the present | past simple (knew / had / was) | Priya wishes she spoke Italian. (she doesn't) |
| the present | past continuous | I wish I was lying on a beach right now. (I'm not) |
| the past | past perfect (had done) | I wish I hadn't lent Omar my bike. (I did lend it) |

- I wish this flat had a balcony. (but it doesn't)
- Do you ever wish you had studied medicine? (you didn't)

After *wish*, both *I wish I was* and *I wish I were* are acceptable.

## C — wish … could / could have

- *I wish I could (do)* = I regret that I can't: *I wish I could play the piano.*
- *I wish I could have (done)* = I regret that I couldn't: *The concert was amazing — I wish I could have been there.*

## D — wish … would / wouldn't

*I wish something would happen* = I want it to happen or change, often as a complaint:

- I wish the neighbours would turn their music down.
- I wish you'd tidy up after yourself instead of leaving everything for me.

*I wish … wouldn't* complains about things people do again and again:

- I wish you wouldn't tap your pen during meetings. (= please stop)

## E — would vs. past for states

Use *wish … would* only for **actions/changes** you want. To say how you would like a **situation** to be, use the past, not *would*:

- I wish Leo would call. (= I want him to call — an action)
- I wish Leo was here. ❌ (not *I wish Leo would be here*)
- I wish someone would give me a lift. / I wish I had a car. ❌ (not *I wish I would have a car*)

### Question 37

#### Question
Your colleague is about to sit an important exam tomorrow. You want to express that you want her to do well. Which sentence is correct?

#### Answer
"I hope you do well tomorrow" is correct. *Wish* can take a noun object (*wish somebody luck / success*), but it cannot introduce a clause about a future event you want to happen — for that English uses *hope*. "I wish you do well" is a classic learner error; "I wish you did well" would be a regret about the present (implying she isn't doing well), and "I wish you would do well" would be a complaint about her behaviour.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. I wish you do well tomorrow.
2. I hope you do well tomorrow. (Correct)
3. I wish you did well tomorrow.
4. I wish you would do well tomorrow.

### Question 39

#### Question
Last week Nadia told her boss exactly what she thought of him, and now she has been passed over for promotion. Which sentence correctly expresses her regret?

#### Answer
"I wish I hadn't said that to my boss." A regret about something that happened in the **past** uses *wish* + past perfect (*had / hadn't done*). "I wish I didn't say" uses the past simple, which after *wish* refers to a present situation, so it doesn't fit a finished event. "I wish I wouldn't have said" is not standard English after *wish*. "I wish I haven't said" uses the present perfect, which is never used after *wish* for regrets.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

2. I wish I wouldn't have said that to my boss.

### Question 40

#### Question
Your flatmate leaves dirty dishes in the sink every single day, and it annoys you. Which sentence is the natural way to complain about this repeated habit?

#### Answer
"I wish you wouldn't leave your dishes in the sink." *I wish … wouldn't* is used to complain about things people do repeatedly and to ask them to stop. "I wish you didn't leave" is grammatically possible but describes a state you regret rather than a request for change, and is less natural for a habit you want stopped. "I wish you don't leave" is wrong because the present is not used after *wish*. "I wish you hadn't left" refers to a single past occasion, not a repeated habit.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

2. I wish you don't leave your dishes in the sink.
3. I wish you hadn't left your dishes in the sink.

### Question 41

#### Question
Two friends are talking about a mutual friend, Sofia, who is away in Australia. Which of these sentences is **wrong**, and why?

A. I wish Sofia would write to us more often.
B. I wish Sofia would be here for the party.
C. I wish Sofia was here for the party.
D. I wish Sofia could come to the party.

#### Answer
Sentence B is wrong. *I wish … would* is used to say we want an **action** or **change** to happen (as in A: writing is an action). To say how we would like a **situation** to be — Sofia being here — we use the past (*was/were*), as in C. *I wish … would be* is not used for states. D is correct: *I wish … could* expresses regret that something is not possible.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Sentence A — after *wish* you need *wrote*, not *would write*.
2. Sentence C — you cannot use *was* after *wish*; only *were* is possible.
3. Sentence B — *wish … would* is for actions we want to happen, not for how we want a situation to be; use *I wish Sofia was/were here*. (Correct)
4. Sentence D — *could* cannot follow *wish*; it should be *can*.
