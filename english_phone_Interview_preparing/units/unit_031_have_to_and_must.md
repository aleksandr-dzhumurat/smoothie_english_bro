# Unit 31 — have to and must

# Theory

## A. have to = it is necessary

**I have to do something** means it is necessary or I am obliged to do it. It behaves like an ordinary verb, so it changes for person and tense:

| Present | Past | Future / other |
|---|---|---|
| I / you / we / they **have to** go | I **had to** go | I**'ll have to** go |
| he / she / it **has to** go | she **had to** go | I**'m going to have to** go |
| | | we **might / may have to** go |

- The road ahead is flooded, so drivers **have to** take the long way round.
- Priya can't join us tonight – she **has to** finish a report.
- On Monday my bike broke down and I **had to** walk to the office.
- I **haven't had to** take a day off sick all year.

Because *have to* is a normal verb, questions and negatives in the present and past simple need **do/does/did**:

- What **do** I **have to** bring to the interview? ❌ *What have I to bring?*
- Marco **doesn't have to** start until ten. ❌ *Marco hasn't to start*
- **Did** you **have to** show your passport? – No, nobody asked.

With other modals and future forms: *They can't fix the boiler today, so we'**ll have to** manage without heating. We **may have to** cancel the trip* (= it's possible that this will be necessary).

## B. must and have to

**Must** is close in meaning to *have to*. Both are fine when you give **your own opinion** about what is necessary, or when you recommend something:

- I haven't seen my grandmother for weeks. I **must** visit her. / I **have to** visit her.
- This café is brilliant. You **must** try it. / You **have to** try it. (= I recommend it)

When you describe an **obligation that exists as a fact** (a rule, a job requirement) rather than your own feeling, *have to* is the natural choice, not *must*:

- Nurses **have to** wear a uniform. (a fact, not my opinion)
- Ahmed **has to** travel abroad every month for his job.

**Must** is normal, however, in **written rules and instructions**:

- Passengers **must** show a valid ticket on request.
- Entries **must** be submitted by 30 June.

For the **past**, use **had to**, never *must*:

- The lecture ran late, so I **had to** skip lunch. ❌ *I must skip lunch (yesterday)*

## C. mustn't vs don't have to – completely different

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| You **mustn't** do it | don't do it – it is forbidden / a bad idea | The paint is wet – you **mustn't** touch it. |
| You **don't have to** do it | it isn't necessary (you can if you want) | You **don't have to** reply today; next week is fine. |

- I promised to keep the surprise. I **mustn't** say anything to Dan. (= I must stay silent)
- Guests **don't have to** book in advance, but it's a good idea.

## D. have got to

**Have got to** means the same as *have to*, mostly in informal speech:

- I'**ve got to** be at the dentist at nine. = I **have to** be at the dentist at nine.
- When **has** Leila **got to** leave? = When **does** Leila **have to** leave?

**See also:** must ('You must be tired') ➜ Unit 28; must / mustn't / needn't ➜ Unit 32.

When we describe an obligation that is simply a fact – a rule of the job – we use *have to*, not *must*. *Must* is used mainly when the speaker gives their own opinion or recommendation ("I must call her") or in written rules. "I must start at 7 every day" therefore sounds odd here, and "I have got to start" without *have* is ungrammatical; "I'm having to start" is not the standard way to express a fixed obligation.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Yes, I must start at 7 every day.
2. Yes, I have to start at 7 every day. (Correct)
3. Yes, I got to start at 7 every day.
4. Yes, I must to start at 7 every day.

### Question 41

#### Question
Complete the question a receptionist asks a visitor: "Excuse me, what time ______ leave tomorrow? I'll arrange a taxi."

#### Answer
"What time **do you have to** leave tomorrow?" *Have to* is a normal verb, so present-simple questions are formed with *do/does*: *do you have to …?* The form "have you to leave" is not used in modern English, "must you to leave" wrongly adds *to* after a modal, and "are you have to leave" mixes an auxiliary *be* with a verb that needs *do*.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. what time have you to leave tomorrow?
2. what time must you to leave tomorrow?
3. what time do you have to leave tomorrow? (Correct)
4. what time are you have to leave tomorrow?

### Question 42

#### Question
One of these sentences uses *must* in a way that is wrong or unnatural. Which one, and why?

#### Answer
"The lift was broken, so we must carry the boxes up the stairs" is wrong. The sentence describes the past (*the lift was broken*), and *must* has no past form for obligation – we say *we **had to** carry the boxes*. The other sentences are fine: "I must remember to water the plants" is the speaker's own opinion about what is necessary; "You must come to dinner one evening" is a recommendation/invitation; "Visitors must sign in at reception" is a written-style rule, where *must* is standard.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. I must remember to water the plants before I leave.
2. You must come to dinner one evening – we'd love to see you.
3. The lift was broken, so we must carry the boxes up the stairs. (Correct)
4. Visitors must sign in at reception.

### Question 43

#### Question
Choose the sentence in which the speaker means "it isn't necessary, but you can if you want".

#### Answer
"You don't have to bring anything – there'll be plenty of food." *Don't have to* = it is not necessary (you are free to do it or not). *Mustn't* has the opposite force: "You mustn't bring anything" would mean *don't bring anything – it's not allowed*. "You haven't to bring" is not a correct negative of *have to*, and "You must bring nothing" is an awkward way of forbidding, not of saying something is unnecessary.

### Question 44

#### Question

#### Answer
"You **mustn't** run near the pool." *Mustn't* means *don't do it – it's forbidden or dangerous*. "You don't have to run" would only mean running isn't necessary, which is not a rule at all. "You haven't got to run" is the informal equivalent of *don't have to* – again "not necessary", not "forbidden". "You must not to run" wrongly adds *to* after the modal *must*.
