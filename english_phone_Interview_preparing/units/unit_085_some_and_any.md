# Unit 85 — some and any

# Theory

## A Positive → some, negative → any

As a general rule, **some** (somebody, someone, something, somewhere) goes with positive sentences and **any** (anybody, anything, anywhere) with negative ones:

| some (positive) | any (negative) |
|---|---|
| I bought some bread. | I didn’t buy any bread. |
| There’s someone in the garden. | There isn’t anyone in the garden. |
| She wants something to drink. | She doesn’t want anything to drink. |

**any** also appears in sentences that are negative in meaning even without *not*:
- He left the house without any keys. (= he didn’t take any keys)
- They refused to tell us anything.
- The test was easy – hardly anyone got a low mark. (= almost nobody)

## B some and any in questions

Both are possible in questions, with different implications.

**some** – when we expect or assume the thing exists, and when we **offer** or **ask for** something:
- Are you looking for someone? (I assume you are)
- Could I have some more rice, please?
- Would you like something to read on the train?

**any** – in most other questions, when we do not know whether the thing exists:
- Do you have any brothers or sisters? (maybe yes, maybe no)
- Is there anyone at reception?

## C if + any

We use **any** after *if*:
- Let me know if you have any problems.
- If anybody calls, tell them I’m out.

Some sentences carry a hidden *if* and take **any**:
- I apologise for any inconvenience. (= if I have caused any inconvenience)
- Anyone who witnessed the incident should contact the police. (= if there is anyone who …)

## D any = it doesn’t matter which

**any** can mean "whichever one you like – it makes no difference":
- Take any seat you want. They’re all free.
- Call me any time after six.
- Anybody could have opened that door – it wasn’t locked.

Compare some- and any- in a conversation:
- "I fancy something sweet." "What exactly?" "Anything – I’m not fussy." (= it doesn’t matter what)
- "Let’s go somewhere this weekend." "Where?" "Anywhere. I just need a break."

## E somebody / anybody are singular, but take they/them/their

These words take a singular verb:
- Someone has left the tap running.

But we refer back with **they / them / their** to avoid *he or she*:
- Somebody has left their bag on the bus.
- If anyone needs a lift, they can ask me.

**See also:** Unit 86 (not … any), Unit 88 (some of / any of), Unit 101C (hardly any).

### Question 38

#### Question
A waiter approaches your table. Which question is the most natural way of offering a drink?

#### Answer
**"Would you like something to drink?"** In offers and requests we normally use *some/something*, because the thing offered exists. *Anything* is possible in a neutral question but sounds less like an offer. *Nothing* would make the sentence negative. *Any thing* as two words is not the correct form.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Would you like anything to drink?
2. Would you like nothing to drink?
3. Would you like something to drink? (Correct)
4. Would you like any thing to drink?

### Question 40

#### Question
Which sentence is wrong, and why?

A) Someone has left their coat in the hall.
B) If anybody wants to join, they’re welcome.
C) Somebody have forgotten to lock the door.
D) Hardly anyone came to the rehearsal.

#### Answer
**C is wrong.** *Somebody* is a singular word and takes a singular verb: **Somebody has forgotten to lock the door.** A and B are correct: *someone/anybody* take a singular verb but *their/they* are used to refer back. D correctly uses *anyone* after *hardly*, which gives negative meaning.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. A — *their* should be *his*, because *someone* is singular.
2. B — *anybody* should be *somebody* after *if*.
3. C — *somebody* is singular and needs *has*, not *have*. (Correct)
4. D — *anyone* should be *someone* in a positive sentence.

### Question 41

#### Question
Why is *any* (not *some*) the correct choice in this sentence? "I’m sorry for any confusion my email caused."

#### Answer
Because the sentence contains a hidden *if*: it means "if my email caused any confusion". The speaker does not know whether there was confusion, so *any* is right, just as after *if* itself. *Some confusion* would assert that confusion definitely happened. The sentence is not a question or offer, and *any* here does not mean "it doesn’t matter which".

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Because *any* is always used after *sorry*.
2. Because the sentence has the idea of *if* – "if my email caused any confusion". (Correct)
3. Because *any* means "it doesn’t matter which confusion".
4. Because apologies are questions in disguise, and questions take *any*.
