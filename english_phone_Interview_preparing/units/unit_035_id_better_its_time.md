# Unit 35 — I’d better … / it’s time …

# Theory

## A. had better (I'd better / you'd better …)

**I'd better do something** = it is advisable to do it; if I don't, there will be a problem or a danger.

- The last train is at 11.40. We**'d better** leave now or we'll miss it.
- "Shall I save a copy of this file?" "Yes, you**'d better**. The system keeps crashing."
- The milk smells odd. You**'d better not** drink it.

Key points about the form:

| point | example |
|---|---|
| **'d = had** (not *would*) | I**'d better** call = I **had better** call |
| tag question uses **hadn't** | We'd better hurry, **hadn't** we? |
| meaning is **present/future**, not past | I'd better book **today / next week**. |
| followed by **bare infinitive** | You'd better **check** ❌ *You'd better to check* |
| negative: **'d better not** | I'd better **not** stay long. |

## B. had better vs should

Both give advice, but they are not interchangeable:

- **had better** is only for a **specific situation**, and implies **trouble if the advice is ignored**.
- **should** works for **general** advice and for **any** situation, with no threat implied.

| situation | had better | should |
|---|---|---|
| specific, with a risk | Your flight boards in ten minutes. You**'d better** run. | You **should** run. (also fine) |
| general habit / opinion | ❌ *You'd better eat more vegetables.* | You **should** eat more vegetables. |
| no danger if ignored | ❌ *That's a great novel – you'd better read it.* | You **should** read it. |

## C. it's time …

You can say **It's time (for somebody) to do something**:

- It's time **to leave**. / It's time **for the children to leave**.

You can also use **it's time + subject + past tense**. The meaning is **present**, not past:

- It's nearly midnight. It's time we **went** home.
- The guests will be here soon. It's time the table **was laid**. ❌ *It's time the table is laid*

**It's time somebody did something** often means they **should already have done it** – it is used to criticise or complain:

- You've been promising to fix that tap for weeks. It's time you **did** it.
- Our neighbours play music till 3 a.m. It's time someone **complained**.

**It's about time …** makes the criticism a little stronger:

- He's thirty-five and still lives with his parents. It's about time he **found** his own place.

### Question 35

#### Question
Your flatmate hasn't done the washing-up for a week. You want to complain that he should have done it already. Which sentence is correct?

#### Answer
"It's time you **did** the washing-up." After *It's time + subject*, we use a **past tense** form with present meaning; the structure often criticises someone for not having done something yet. "It's time you do" is a common learner error – the present tense is not used here. "It's time you to do" mixes two patterns: *It's time for you to do* would be possible, but without *for* it is wrong. "It's time you have done" is not a possible form after *it's time*.

### Question 36

#### Question
Read: "It's time the company **replaced** its ancient computers." What does the past tense *replaced* tell us about when the replacing should happen?

#### Answer
The past form after *it's time* refers to **now**, not the past. The sentence means the company should replace the computers now – and, since *it's time somebody did something* is typically used to criticise, it suggests they should already have done so. It does not mean the computers were replaced in the past, nor that this is a hypothetical future condition, and there is nothing ungrammatical about the past tense here – it is the required form.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. The company replaced the computers at some point in the past.
2. It refers to the present: the company should replace them now and arguably should have done so already. (Correct)
3. The past tense makes the sentence a hypothetical condition about the future.
4. The sentence is a mistake; it should read "It's time the company replaces its computers".
