# Unit 29 — may and might 1

# Theory

## A. may / might for present possibility

Suppose you're looking for your colleague Jonah. Nobody is certain where he is, so people guess:

- He **may be** in the canteen. (= perhaps he is in the canteen)
- He **might be** on a call. (= perhaps he is on a call)
- Ask Priya – she **might know**. (= perhaps she knows)

*May* and *might* both say that something is **possible**. In this meaning they are interchangeable:

- It **may be** a mistake. / It **might be** a mistake. (= perhaps it is a mistake)

The negatives are **may not** and **might not**:

- The shop **may not be** open yet. (= perhaps it isn't open)
- They **might not remember** you. (= perhaps they don't remember)

Structure:

| Subject | Modal | Verb form | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| I / you / he / they … | **may** / **might** (not) | **be** + adj/noun/place | She **may be** upstairs. |
| | | **be -ing** | He **might be sleeping**. |
| | | other infinitive | They **may not know**. |

Don't confuse **may be** (two words: modal + verb) with **maybe** (one word: an adverb meaning "perhaps"):

- The train **may be** late. (may + be)
- "Is the train late?" "**Maybe**. Let's check." (= perhaps)

## B. may have / might have for the past

For possibility in the **past**, use **may have** / **might have** + past participle:

- "Why didn't Zara come to the party?" "She **may have been** working late." (= perhaps she was working late)
- "I can't find my glasses." "You **might have left** them in the car." (= perhaps you left them there)
- "Why wasn't Luis at the briefing?" "He **might not have known** about it." (= perhaps he didn't know)
- "Sam seemed very quiet." "He **may not have been feeling** well."

Structure:

| Subject | Modal | Verb form | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| I / you / she / they … | **may** / **might** (not) **have** | **been** + adj/place | She **may have been** ill. |
| | | **been -ing** | He **might have been driving**. |
| | | past participle | They **may not have heard**. |

## C. could is similar – but couldn't is not

*Could* often means the same as *may/might*:

- It's an odd claim, but it **could be** true. (= it is possible that it's true)
- You **could have left** your glasses in the car. (= perhaps you left them there)

But the **negative** is different. **Couldn't** means "not possible", whereas **may not / might not** mean "possibly not":

- Nina **couldn't have received** the invitation – otherwise she'd have replied. (= it is **not possible** that she received it)
- Nina hasn't replied. She **might not have received** the invitation. (= **perhaps** she didn't – we don't know)

Common errors:

- ❌ He may to be busy. ✅ He **may be** busy.
- ❌ She might had forgotten. ✅ She **might have forgotten**.
- ❌ It maybe true. ✅ It **may be** true.

### Question 28

#### Question
Compare: (a) "Dan **might not have seen** the message." (b) "Dan **couldn't have seen** the message." Which explanation of the difference is correct?

#### Answer
(a) *might not have seen* means it is **possible** that he didn't see it – perhaps he did, perhaps he didn't. (b) *couldn't have seen* means it is **not possible** that he saw it – the speaker is sure he didn't (for example, because his phone was switched off). *Couldn't* is therefore **not** simply a synonym for *might not*; the negative of *could* behaves differently from the negative of *may/might*.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Both mean the same – "perhaps he didn't see it".
2. (a) means perhaps he didn't see it; (b) means it is not possible that he saw it. (Correct)
3. (a) is about the past; (b) is about the present.
4. (b) is ungrammatical – *couldn't* cannot be followed by *have*.

#### Question

#### Answer
"She **may not have been feeling** well." #### Correct variant

2. She can't have been feeling well.
