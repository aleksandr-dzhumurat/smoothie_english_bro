# Unit 52 — Question tags (do you? isn’t it? etc.)

# Theory

## A What a question tag is

A question tag is a mini-question attached to the end of a statement: *You've met Omar before, **haven't you**?* / *The film was terrible, **wasn't it**?*

The tag is built from an **auxiliary verb** + a **pronoun**. Use the auxiliary that is already in the sentence (*have, was, will, can, should* …). If the sentence is present simple or past simple, there is no auxiliary in it, so the tag uses **do/does/did**:

| statement | tag |
|---|---|
| Nina speaks Portuguese, | **doesn't she**? |
| You forgot your umbrella, | **didn't you**? |
| They can't hear us, | **can they**? |
| It's been a long day, | **hasn't it**? |

## B Positive + negative, negative + positive

The normal pattern is opposite polarity:

| positive statement + **negative** tag | negative statement + **positive** tag |
|---|---|
| Amir will help us, **won't he**? | Amir won't mind, **will he**? |
| There's a bank nearby, **isn't there**? | There isn't much time, **is there**? |
| We should book now, **shouldn't we**? | You haven't finished, **have you**? |

Be careful with *yes/no* answers to a negative statement: to 'You aren't coming tonight, are you?', **'Yes'** means *Yes, I am coming*, and **'No'** means *No, I'm not coming*. The answer describes the fact, not the tag.

## C Meaning depends on intonation

- **Voice goes down** → not a real question; you expect agreement: *Lovely weather, isn't it?* / *That was a boring lecture, wasn't it?*
- **Voice goes up** → a genuine question: *You haven't seen my glasses, have you?* (= Have you seen my glasses?)

A **negative statement + positive tag** with rising intonation is a polite way to ask for something or request help:

- *You couldn't lend me a pen, could you?*
- *You don't happen to know when the shop closes, do you?*

## D Special tags

| after … | tag | example |
|---|---|---|
| Let's … | **shall we?** | Let's order pizza, shall we? |
| Don't … (imperative) | **will you?** | Don't forget the tickets, will you? |
| I'm … | **aren't I?** | I'm next in the queue, aren't I? |

❌ ~~I'm late, amn't I?~~ ✅ I'm late, **aren't I**? — ❌ ~~Let's start, don't we?~~ ✅ Let's start, **shall we**?

### Question 32

#### Question
Complete the sentence with the correct tag: "Your brother works in a hospital, ______?"

#### Answer
**"doesn't he?"** The statement is present simple with a third-person singular subject (*works*), so the tag needs *does* + *n't* + *he*. The statement is positive, so the tag must be negative. "Isn't he?" is wrong because the sentence has no form of *be* — learners often reach for *be* when there is no visible auxiliary, but present simple takes *do/does*. "Doesn't your brother?" repeats the noun instead of using a pronoun. "Does he?" has the wrong polarity for a positive statement.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. isn't he?
2. doesn't he? (Correct)
3. doesn't your brother?
4. does he?

### Question 33

#### Question
You are suggesting to a friend that you both take a break. Which sentence is correct?

#### Answer
**"Let's take a break, shall we?"** After *Let's …* the fixed tag is *shall we?* "Don't we" would be the tag for a present simple statement (*We take a break at eleven, don't we?*), "will we" is not used after *Let's*, and "shan't we" wrongly applies the opposite-polarity rule to an imperative suggestion, which follows its own pattern.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Let's take a break, don't we?
2. Let's take a break, will we?
3. Let's take a break, shall we? (Correct)
4. Let's take a break, shan't we?

### Question 34

#### Question
Your neighbour asks, with falling intonation: "You're not working this weekend, are you?" You will in fact be working on Saturday. Which reply is correct?

#### Answer
**"Yes, I am."** With a negative statement, *yes* confirms the positive fact (I am working) and *no* confirms the negative fact (I am not working). Since you are working, you answer *Yes, I am*. "No, I am" is contradictory — *no* agrees that you are not working, but then *I am* says you are. "Yes, I'm not" is the mirror-image contradiction. "No, I'm not" is grammatical but gives the wrong information because it means you are free this weekend.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. No, I am.
2. Yes, I'm not.
3. No, I'm not.
4. Yes, I am. (Correct)

### Question 35

#### Question
You need a lift to the airport tomorrow and you think your flatmate might be free. Which is the most natural way to ask, using a question tag?

#### Answer
**"You couldn't give me a lift to the airport tomorrow, could you?"** To ask for a favour politely, English uses a *negative statement + positive tag* with rising intonation. "You could give me a lift, couldn't you?" with a positive statement sounds like you are assuming the answer, which is pushy rather than polite. "You couldn't give me a lift, couldn't you?" breaks the polarity rule (negative + negative). "Could you give me a lift, could you?" is not a statement plus tag — it is a question with an impossible second question attached.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. You couldn't give me a lift to the airport tomorrow, could you? (Correct)
2. You could give me a lift to the airport tomorrow, couldn't you?
3. You couldn't give me a lift to the airport tomorrow, couldn't you?
4. Could you give me a lift to the airport tomorrow, could you?
