# Unit 40 — if I had known … I wish I had known …

# Theory

## A. if + had done for the past

Imagine that Nina's colleague Mark had a birthday last week, but Nina only found out afterwards. She says: **If I'd known it was your birthday, I would have baked something.** She didn't know, so she didn't bake – *if I'd known* (= *if I had known*) tells us this.

To imagine a **past** situation that did not happen, use **if + past perfect** (*if I'd known / if you'd been / if they hadn't left*) and **would have + past participle** in the main clause:

- I didn't recognise you in the queue. **If I'd recognised** you, I **would have waved**.
- We stayed in last night. We **would have gone** to the concert **if the tickets hadn't sold out**.
- **If you'd been paying** attention, you **wouldn't have missed** the turning. (but you weren't)
- The sunset was stunning. I **would have taken** a photo **if I'd had** my phone with me. (= if I had had)

Compare present and past unreal conditions:

| now (Unit 39) | past (this unit) |
|---|---|
| I'm not cold. **If I was** cold, I**'d put** on a jumper. | I wasn't cold. **If I'd been** cold, I**'d have put** on a jumper. |

## B. no would have in the if-clause; the two meanings of 'd

Do **not** say *if something would have happened*. *Would* belongs in the other part:

- If I'**d seen** the sign, I **would have stopped**. ❌ *If I would have seen the sign*

The short form **'d** can mean **had** or **would** – check which by what follows:

| 'd = had | 'd = would |
|---|---|
| If I**'d seen** the sign … (**had** + past participle) | I**'d have stopped**. (**would** + have + past participle) |

## C. wish + had done

*Wish* takes the past perfect in the same way, to express regret about the **past**:

- **I wish something had happened** = I'm sorry it didn't happen.
- **I wish something hadn't happened** = I'm sorry it happened.

- I wish I**'d learnt** to swim as a child. (I didn't)
- My head hurts. I wish I **hadn't stayed** up so late. (I did)
- Do you ever wish you**'d taken** that job in Madrid? (you didn't)

Compare *glad* and *wish*:

- I'm glad I **kept** the receipt. (= I kept it)
- I wish I**'d kept** the receipt. (= I didn't keep it)

We do **not** say *wish … would have*: ❌ *I wish the food would have been hotter* → ✅ *I wish the food **had been** hotter.*

## D. would (do) vs would have (done); could / might have

The *if*-clause can be past while the result is present or past:

- If I**'d taken** the night train, I **would be** exhausted now. (present result – I'm not exhausted)
- If I**'d taken** the night train, I **would have arrived** this morning. (past result – I didn't)

In the main clause, *would have* can be replaced by **could have** (= would have been able to) or **might have** (= perhaps would have):

- If the shop hadn't closed early, we **would have bought** the cake. / we **could have bought** it. / we **might have bought** it.

**See also:** had done ➜ Unit 15; if I do / if I did ➜ Unit 38; if I knew / I wish I knew ➜ Unit 39; wish ➜ Unit 41.

### Question 24

#### Question
Yesterday Elena missed an important call because her phone was on silent. Which sentence correctly imagines a different outcome?

#### Answer
"**If her phone hadn't been** on silent, **she would have answered** the call." For an imagined past situation we use *if + past perfect* and *would have + past participle*. "If her phone wasn't on silent, she would answer" refers to the present, but the event is over. "If her phone wouldn't have been on silent" wrongly puts *would have* in the *if*-clause. "If her phone hadn't been on silent, she would answer" mixes a past condition with a present result – possible in some contexts, but here the answering would have happened yesterday, so *would have answered* is needed.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. If her phone wasn't on silent, she would answer the call.
2. If her phone hadn't been on silent, she would have answered the call. (Correct)
3. If her phone wouldn't have been on silent, she would have answered the call.
4. If her phone hadn't been on silent, she would answer the call.

### Question 26

#### Question
In the sentence "If I'd checked the weather, I'd have brought a coat", what do the two *'d* forms stand for?

#### Answer
The first *'d* (*I'd checked*) is **had** – it is followed by a past participle and forms the past perfect in the *if*-clause. The second *'d* (*I'd have brought*) is **would** – it is followed by *have* + past participle, forming *would have brought* in the main clause. They are not both *would* (that would wrongly put *would* in the *if*-clause), nor both *had* (*had have brought* is impossible), and *'d* is never short for *did*.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Both stand for *would*.
2. Both stand for *had*.
3. The first is *had*; the second is *would*. (Correct)
4. The first is *did*; the second is *had*.

### Question 27

#### Question
Compare: (a) "If Sam had caught the early train, he would have been at the meeting." (b) "If Sam had caught the early train, he would be at the meeting." Which statement is true?

#### Answer
Both sentences have a **past** condition (he didn't catch the early train), but the results differ in time. In (a), *would have been* is a **past** result – the meeting is over and he wasn't there. In (b), *would be* is a **present** result – the meeting is happening now and he isn't there. Both are correct; the choice depends on whether the result is past or present. It is not true that (b) is ungrammatical, that (a) refers to the present, or that (b) has a present condition.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. (b) is incorrect – after a past-perfect *if*-clause the main clause must use *would have*.
2. (a) has a past result (the meeting is over); (b) has a present result (the meeting is happening now). Both are correct. (Correct)
3. (a) refers to the present and (b) to the past.
4. (b) means Sam did catch the early train and is at the meeting.

### Question 28

#### Question
Read: "If the road hadn't been closed, we **might have arrived** on time." Which explanation of *might have arrived* is correct?

#### Answer
*Might have arrived* means "**perhaps we would have arrived**" – the speaker is not certain that an open road would have guaranteed arriving on time, only that it was possible. In the main clause of a past unreal condition, *would have* expresses a definite imagined result, *could have* means "would have been able to", and *might have* means "would perhaps have". It is not a statement of permission, nor does it mean the speakers actually arrived on time.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. It means "we were allowed to arrive on time".
2. It means "we definitely would have arrived on time".
3. It means "we did arrive on time, despite the closed road".
4. It means "perhaps we would have arrived on time" – a possible, not certain, imagined result. (Correct)
