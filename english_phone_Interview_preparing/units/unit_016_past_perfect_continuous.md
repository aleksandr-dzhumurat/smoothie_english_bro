# Unit 16 — Past perfect continuous (I had been doing)

# Theory

## A Form and meaning

Situation: Leila came downstairs one morning and saw puddles all over the patio, although the sky was clear. It **had been raining** during the night. It wasn't raining when she looked out — but it had been raining **before** that moment.

*had been raining* is the **past perfect continuous**: **had been + -ing**.

| Subject | had been | -ing |
|---|---|---|
| I / you / we / they (= I'd …) | had been | working / waiting / driving … |
| he / she / it (= he'd …) | had been | working / waiting / driving … |

We use it for an activity that was in progress **up to** (or shortly before) a moment in the past, often to explain a past situation:

- Oskar's eyes were red because he**'d been chopping** onions.
- Priya was exhausted when she got in. She**'d been rehearsing** all evening.
- I stayed with a cousin in Buenos Aires. He **hadn't been living** there long, but he already knew every café in the neighbourhood.

It also says how long something had been happening before something else occurred:

- We**'d been hiking** for about two hours when the storm broke.
- The band **had been playing** for ten minutes when the power went off.

## B Present perfect continuous vs past perfect continuous

The only difference is the viewpoint: *have been -ing* looks back from **now**; *had been -ing* looks back from a **past moment**.

| Present perfect continuous | Past perfect continuous |
|---|---|
| I hope the taxi comes soon. I**'ve been standing** here for half an hour. (up to now) | The taxi finally came. I**'d been standing** there for half an hour. (up to the moment it came) |
| Tariq is sweating. He**'s been jogging**. | Tariq was sweating. He**'d been jogging**. |

## C Past continuous vs past perfect continuous

- *was -ing* = in progress **at** that past moment.
- *had been -ing* = in progress **before** that moment, usually finished by then.

- When we set off, it **wasn't raining**. But it **had been raining**, so the paths were muddy.
- Farah **was lying** on the sofa. She was worn out because she**'d been cleaning** the whole flat.

Compare: *When I arrived, Ben **was talking** on the phone.* (still talking) / *When I arrived, Ben **had been talking** on the phone for an hour.* (that's why the line was busy earlier — he may or may not still be talking).

## D Verbs not used in continuous forms

Some verbs — *know, like, believe, belong, have* (possession) and others — are not normally used in continuous forms, so we use the **past perfect simple** even for a long period:

- We were close. We **had known** each other since primary school. ❌ (not *had been knowing*)
- When Arjun shaved his head, everyone was shocked, because he**'d always had** long hair. ❌ (not *he'd been having*)

### Question 34

#### Question
Compare: "When I got to the office, Anna **was working** on the report." / "When I got to the office, Anna **had been working** on the report." Which statement about the difference is correct?

#### Answer
In the first, Anna was in the middle of working at the moment I arrived. In the second, she had spent time working on it **before** I arrived — perhaps she had just finished, or perhaps we are stressing the period leading up to my arrival. The past continuous describes an action in progress at a point in time; the past perfect continuous describes an action in progress before that point. Neither sentence is ungrammatical, and they are not simply formal/informal variants.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. The first sentence describes what was happening at that moment; the second describes an activity that had been going on before that moment. (Correct)
2. The two sentences mean exactly the same thing; the second is more formal.
3. The second sentence is wrong because "when" cannot be used with the past perfect continuous.
4. The first means Anna had finished the report; the second means she was still working on it.
