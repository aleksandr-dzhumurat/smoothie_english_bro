# Unit 44 — Passive 3

# Theory

## A — Verbs with two objects: I was offered … / we were given …

Some verbs take **two objects** — a person and a thing: *give, offer, pay, show, tell, ask, send, teach*.

- The manager offered **Layla** (object 1) **a promotion** (object 2).

Each object can become the subject of a passive sentence:

| Starting with the person | Starting with the thing |
|---|---|
| Layla was offered a promotion. | A promotion was offered to Layla. |
| We were told the truth. | The truth was told to us. |

In practice, English **usually begins with the person**:

- I've been given a second chance. (= somebody has given me a second chance)
- You'll be sent a confirmation email.
- Nobody was told about the road closure.
- She's paid well for a job she enjoys.

## B — being done: the passive -ing form

The passive of *doing / seeing* is **being done / being seen**. Use it wherever an -ing form is needed — after verbs like *like, hate, remember, avoid, mind* and after prepositions.

- I can't stand being ignored. (= people ignoring me)
- Ravi remembers being carried on his father's shoulders at a parade.
- We got into the concert without being asked for tickets.
- She's afraid of being laughed at.

## C — I was born …

*Be born* is always passive, and about a person's birth it is always **past**:

- I was born in Lagos. ❌ *I am born in Lagos.*
- Where were your parents born? ❌ *Where are your parents born?*

Present forms only appear for general statements: *Around 350,000 babies are born every day.*

## D — get as a passive auxiliary

In informal spoken English **get** can replace *be* in the passive, but only for things that **happen** — events, not states:

- Nobody got hurt in the crash. (= nobody was hurt)
- My phone got stolen on the train.
- She didn't get chosen for the team.

Not for states or feelings that "don't happen":

- ❌ Our teacher gets respected by everyone. ✅ Our teacher **is** respected by everyone.
- ❌ Very little got understood about the disease. ✅ Very little **was** understood …

*Be* is always safe; *get* is optional and informal.

*Get* also appears in fixed expressions that aren't really passive: **get married, get divorced, get dressed, get changed, get lost** (= not know where you are).

### Question 34

#### Question
Someone gave Tomasz a bicycle for his birthday. Which passive sentence is the most natural way to say this in English?

#### Answer
"Tomasz was given a bicycle for his birthday." *Give* has two objects (Tomasz, a bicycle) and English normally begins the passive with the **person**. "A bicycle was given to Tomasz" is also grammatical but less usual. "Tomasz was given to a bicycle" is wrong because *to* marks the receiver, and Tomasz is the receiver, not the thing given. "Tomasz gave a bicycle" is active and reverses the meaning.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Tomasz was given to a bicycle for his birthday.
2. Tomasz was given a bicycle for his birthday. (Correct)
3. Tomasz gave a bicycle for his birthday.
4. Tomasz was give a bicycle for his birthday.

#### Question
Complete: "Most people don't enjoy ___ what to do all the time."

#### Answer
"Most people don't enjoy **being told** what to do all the time." "Telling" is active and would mean the people tell others. "To be told" is wrong because *enjoy* is followed by -ing, not the infinitive. "Been told" cannot follow *enjoy* — *been* needs *have* before it.

### Question 36

#### Question
Which sentence contains a mistake with *born*?

A. My grandmother was born in a small fishing village.
B. Hundreds of children are born in this hospital every month.
C. Where are you born?
D. Both my brothers were born in December.

#### Answer
Sentence C is wrong. When we talk about a specific person's birth, *be born* is always in the past: "Where **were** you born?" The present *are born* (as in B) is only used for general, repeated facts such as how many babies are born every month. A and D are correct past passives.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Sentence A — *born* needs an agent: *was born by my mother*.
2. Sentence B — *are born* is impossible; it must always be *were born*.
3. Sentence D — *were born* should be *had been born*.
4. Sentence C — a person's birth is always past, so it must be *Where were you born?* (Correct)

### Question 37

#### Question
In which sentence is *get* used correctly as a passive auxiliary?

#### Answer
"My wallet got stolen at the festival." *Get* replaces *be* in the passive only when something **happens** — an event like a theft — and mainly in informal speech. "This museum gets visited by thousands of tourists" describes a habitual state rather than a happening and sounds unnatural; *is visited* is needed. "The painting gets known all over the world" is wrong because *known* is a state, not an event. "Nobody got understand the instructions" uses the base form instead of a past participle and isn't a passive at all.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. This museum gets visited by thousands of tourists.
2. My wallet got stolen at the festival. (Correct)
3. The painting gets known all over the world.
4. Nobody got understand the instructions.

### Question 38

#### Question
A job applicant says: "I ___ the position, but I turned it down." Which option is correct, and why is the alternative with *to* less natural?

#### Answer
"I **was offered** the position, but I turned it down." *Offer* is a two-object verb; the passive normally starts with the person who receives the offer, so *I was offered the position*. "The position was offered to me" is grammatical but less common as a way to talk about oneself. "I was offered to the position" wrongly adds *to* before the thing offered. "I offered the position" is active and would mean the speaker offered the job to someone else.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. I was offered to the position, but I turned it down.
2. I offered the position, but I turned it down.
3. I was offering the position, but I turned it down.
4. I was offered the position, but I turned it down. (Correct)
