# Unit 104 — quite, pretty, rather and fairly

# Theory

## A quite and pretty

*Quite* and *pretty* both mean "more than a little, but less than very":

- The new café is quite busy at lunchtime. / … is pretty busy at lunchtime.
- My office is quite close to the station, so I walk there pretty often.

*Pretty* is informal and belongs mainly to spoken English.

**Word order with a/an:** *quite* goes **before** the article, *pretty* goes **after** it:

| quite | pretty |
|---|---|
| quite a long meeting | a pretty long meeting |
| quite an interesting idea | a pretty interesting idea |

❌ a quite long meeting ✅ quite a long meeting

Only *quite* (not *pretty*) can be used in these patterns:

- *quite a/an + noun* (no adjective): *The result was quite a shock.* (= a big shock)
- *quite a lot (of)*: *There were quite a lot of complaints.*
- *quite + verb*, especially *like / enjoy*: *I quite enjoy cooking, but I wouldn't call it a hobby.*

## B rather

*Rather* is similar in strength to *quite/pretty*, but we tend to use it for **negative** ideas, things we are not happy about:

- The soup is rather salty.
- Ahmed is rather disorganised; he keeps losing documents.

(*Quite* would also be possible in these sentences.) When *rather* is used with a **positive** idea, it suggests "surprisingly" or "unusually":

- This cheap wine is rather good, actually.

## C fairly

*Fairly* is **weaker** than *quite/rather/pretty*. If something is *fairly good*, it is acceptable but could be better:

- The hotel was fairly clean, but I've stayed in nicer places.
- I speak French fairly well — enough to order dinner, not enough to argue.

## D quite = completely

With certain adjectives *quite* means "completely", not "moderately". These are mostly adjectives that describe an absolute state:

*sure, certain, right, wrong, true, safe, clear, obvious, different, unnecessary, incredible, extraordinary, amazing, impossible*

- "Is this the right platform?" "Yes, I'm quite sure." (= completely sure)
- The finished house was quite different from the drawings. (= completely different)

Also with some verbs: *I quite agree.* (= I completely agree)

*Not quite* = not completely: *I'm not quite ready.* / *"Have you finished?" "Not quite."*

Compare:

- The talk was quite interesting. (= moderately interesting)
- What he said was quite wrong. (= completely wrong)

## Extra practice

### Question 33

#### Question
Which sentence is correct?

#### Answer
The correct sentence is *We stayed in quite an old hotel near the harbour.* With *quite*, the word order is *quite + a/an + adjective + noun*; the article comes after *quite*, not before it. *A quite old hotel* is a typical learner error. *Quite old a hotel* is not a possible order. *An old quite hotel* separates the adjective from *quite*, which is also wrong.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. We stayed in a quite old hotel near the harbour.
2. We stayed in quite an old hotel near the harbour. (Correct)
3. We stayed in quite old a hotel near the harbour.
4. We stayed in an old quite hotel near the harbour.

### Question 34

#### Question
Compare: "Your answer is quite good" and "Your answer is quite right". What does *quite* mean in each sentence?

#### Answer
In *quite good*, *quite* means "more than a little, but less than very" — the answer is reasonably good. In *quite right*, *quite* means "completely" — the answer is 100% correct. *Right* belongs to a group of absolute adjectives (*right, wrong, true, sure, different, impossible*, etc.) with which *quite* takes the meaning "completely". Treating both uses as "moderately" would give the odd meaning "moderately right".

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. In both sentences *quite* means "moderately"; the two sentences have the same strength.
2. In both sentences *quite* means "completely"; the answer is perfect in both cases.
3. In *quite good* it means "moderately", but in *quite right* it means "completely". (Correct)
4. In *quite good* it means "completely", but in *quite right* it means "moderately".

### Question 35

#### Question
A tourist is describing her hotel room: "The room was ______ big, but I would have preferred a larger one." Which word best fits the meaning "acceptable but could be better"?

#### Answer
The correct word is *fairly*. *Fairly* is the weakest of the four adverbs and signals that something is only moderately satisfactory — exactly the meaning of "could be better". *Quite* and *pretty* would be stronger and would sit less naturally with the wish for a larger room. *Rather* usually carries a negative or "surprisingly" flavour, and *rather big* would suggest the room was unexpectedly or too big, which contradicts the second half of the sentence.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. rather
2. fairly (Correct)
3. pretty
4. quite

### Question 36

#### Question
Which of these sentences is NOT possible?

#### Answer
The impossible sentence is *There were pretty a lot of people at the concert.* *Pretty* cannot be used in the patterns *quite a lot (of)*, *quite a + noun* or *quite + verb*; those belong to *quite* only. The other sentences are all acceptable: *quite a lot of people* is the standard pattern, *a pretty big crowd* has *pretty* correctly after the article, and *I quite like* shows *quite* with a verb.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. There were quite a lot of people at the concert.
2. There was a pretty big crowd at the concert.
3. I quite like going to concerts.
4. There were pretty a lot of people at the concert. (Correct)

### Question 37

#### Question
Yusuf tastes some biscuits his neighbour baked and says, "These biscuits are rather good — where's the recipe from?" What does his use of *rather* suggest?

#### Answer
It suggests the biscuits are surprisingly or unusually good. *Rather* is normally used with negative ideas (*rather cold, rather boring*), so when it appears with a positive adjective it adds the sense "better than expected". It does not mean the biscuits are only slightly good — that would be *fairly good*. Nor is it a criticism: the question about the recipe confirms Yusuf is pleased.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. He is being polite but actually thinks the biscuits are only slightly good.
2. He thinks the biscuits are surprisingly or unusually good. (Correct)
3. He is criticising the biscuits, because *rather* always has a negative meaning.
4. He is saying the biscuits are completely good, since *rather* means "completely" like *quite*.
