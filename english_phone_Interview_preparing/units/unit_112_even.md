# Unit 112 — even

# Theory

## A even = surprising / unusual

We use **even** to point to something that is unusual or surprising – more than you would expect:

- Marco eats everything, **even** raw onions. (raw onions are an unusual thing to eat)
- It's a tiny village. **Even** the mayor knows everyone's name.
- She swims in the lake all year, **even** in January.
- I couldn't hear the announcement, **even** with the volume turned up.

**Not even** is the negative form and can also start a sentence:

- Nobody laughed at the joke, **not even** his mother. / **Not even** his mother laughed.

## B even with the verb

**Even** can go in the middle of a sentence, with the verb (see Unit 110 for position):

| subject | (auxiliary) | even | verb | rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ana | has | even | climbed | Kilimanjaro. |
| They | – | even | own | a small island. |

**Even** is common with negatives – *not even / can't even / didn't even*:

- I'm useless in the kitchen. I **can't even** make toast. (making toast is very easy)
- The waiter was rude. He **didn't even** look at us.
- After the race she **wasn't even** tired.

## C even + comparative

**Even** + comparative (*even bigger / even more difficult / even less*) means "more than something that is already big / difficult":

- Yesterday was cold, but today is **even colder**.
- I thought the first exam was hard; the second was **even harder**.
- I had little time, and now I have **even less**.

## D even though / even when / even if

To join two clauses we need **even though / even when / even if** + subject + verb. **Even** alone cannot introduce a clause:

- ✅ **Even though** Lena has a licence, she never drives. ❌ *Even Lena has a licence, …*
- He stays calm **even when** the children are screaming.
- Don't touch the wire, **even if** the power is off.
- ✅ I can't open the jar **even if** I use a cloth. ❌ *even I use a cloth*

Compare **even if** (+ clause) and **even** (+ noun phrase):

- The path is slippery **even if** you have good boots. / The path is slippery, **even for** experienced hikers.

Compare **even if** and **if**:

- We're having the barbecue tomorrow **even if** it rains. (it makes no difference – we'll do it anyway)
- We're having the barbecue tomorrow, but not **if** it rains. (rain will stop us)

**See also:** if and when ➜ Unit 25D; though / even though ➜ Unit 113E

### Question 31

#### Question
Everybody in the office is normally cheerful except Victor, who is famous for being grumpy. Today the whole office was in a good mood, including Victor. Which sentence best reports this?

#### Answer
"Everybody was in a good mood today, **even Victor**." We use *even* to point to the most surprising case: Victor being cheerful is unexpected, so *even* goes directly before his name. *Not even Victor* would mean the opposite (nobody was in a good mood, and Victor least of all). *Even if Victor* introduces a condition, which is not the meaning here, and "even Victor was" placed after the comma with no connection is ungrammatical.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Everybody was in a good mood today, not even Victor.
2. Everybody was in a good mood today, even Victor. (Correct)
3. Everybody was in a good mood today even if Victor.
4. Everybody was in a good mood today, even Victor did.

### Question 34

#### Question
One of these sentences is grammatically wrong. Which one, and why?

#### Answer
Sentence 2 is wrong: "Even she has a map, she keeps getting lost." *Even* alone cannot introduce a clause with a subject and verb; we need *even though* (or *although*): *Even though she has a map, she keeps getting lost.* The other sentences are correct: *even though* + clause (1), *even when* + clause (3), and *even* + prepositional phrase without a verb (4).

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. Even though the tickets were expensive, the concert sold out.
2. Even she has a map, she keeps getting lost. (Correct)
3. He wears sandals even when it snows.
4. The shop is busy even on Sunday mornings.

### Question 35

#### Question
Two friends are planning a trip to the coast. Which sentence means "we will definitely go – bad weather will not stop us"?

#### Answer
"We're going to the coast tomorrow **even if** it's stormy." *Even if* says the condition makes no difference: the trip happens whether or not the weather is bad. "We're going … if it's stormy" would oddly make the storm the reason for going; "we won't go if it's stormy" means the weather will stop us; and "even it's stormy" is ungrammatical because *even* cannot introduce a clause on its own.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. We're going to the coast tomorrow if it's stormy.
2. We won't go to the coast tomorrow if it's stormy.
3. We're going to the coast tomorrow even it's stormy.
4. We're going to the coast tomorrow even if it's stormy. (Correct)
