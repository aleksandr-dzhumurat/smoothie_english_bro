# Unit 24 — will be doing and will have done

# Theory

## A. The picture

Imagine a group of runners waiting at the start line of a marathon at 8.55.

- **In twenty minutes**, the race will have started. Everyone **will be running**.
- **In five hours**, the streets will be quiet. The race **will have finished**. Most runners **will have gone** home.

*Will be doing* = in the middle of an action at a future time. *Will have done* = an action completed before a future time.

## B. will be doing (future continuous)

*I will be doing something* means I will be **in the middle of** doing it at a particular time in the future:

- This time tomorrow I**'ll be sitting** on a plane to Athens.
- Don't bother applying – you**'ll be wasting** your energy.

Compare *will be doing* and *will do*:

- Don't call between six and seven – we**'ll be having** dinner. (in progress at that time)
- Let's wait for Dad to get home, and then we**'ll have** dinner. (a single future action)

The future continuous is the future counterpart of the past and present continuous:

| Time | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Past | was/were doing | At 3 o'clock yesterday, Hana **was** in the library. She **was revising**. |
| Present | am/is/are doing | It's 3 o'clock now. She**'s** in the library. She**'s revising**. |
| Future | will be doing | At 3 o'clock tomorrow, she**'ll be** in the library. She**'ll be revising**. |

## C. will be doing for complete future actions

*Will be -ing* can also describe a **whole** future action, especially planned or scheduled events, with a meaning close to *will do* or *going to do*:

- The council **will be announcing** the new bus routes next week.
- Later in the show, we**'ll be speaking** to the winning team.
- Our goalkeeper is suspended and **won't be playing** on Sunday.

## D. will have done (future perfect)

*I will have done something* = it **will be complete** before a point in the future:

- Nadia leaves the office at 5.30 every day. Don't call her at six – she**'ll have left**.
- Hurry up. By the time we arrive, the concert **will have begun**.

The future perfect sits alongside the present perfect and past perfect:

| Form | Example |
|---|---|
| present perfect | Omar and Lea **have been** married for nine years. |
| future perfect | Next spring they **will have been** married for ten years. |
| past perfect | When they bought their flat, they **had been** married for two years. |

Common error: using the simple future where a completed state is meant.

- ❌ By 2030 the bridge will finish. ✅ By 2030 the bridge **will have been finished**.

**See also:** will → Units 21–22; by then / by the time → Unit 120.

#### Answer

### Question 27

#### Question
Compare: (a) "Don't ring at nine – we'll be eating." (b) "Wait until Lucia gets here, and then we'll eat." What does the contrast show?

#### Answer
(a) uses the future continuous *will be eating* to say the meal will be **in progress** at nine – the caller would interrupt it. #### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

### Question 28

#### Question
Kenji and Aiko got married in 2010. Which sentence about the year 2035 is correct?

#### Answer
"In 2035 they **will have been** married for 25 years." The future perfect *will have been* expresses a state that will be complete (reached) at a future point. *They will be married for 25 years* is not how English expresses a duration reached by a future date. *They have been married for 25 years* is the present perfect and is only true now if 25 years have already passed. *They had been married* is the past perfect, which refers to a time before another past time.

#### Correct variant
