# Unit 143 — Phrasal verbs 7 up (1)

# Theory

## A movement towards somebody: go up, come up, catch up, keep up

- **go up / come up / walk up (to …)** = approach somebody or something: *A stranger walked up to me at the bus stop and asked for directions.*
- **catch up (with somebody)** or **catch somebody up** = go faster than the people ahead of you until you reach them: *Start without me — I'll catch up with you / I'll catch you up at the café.* Also used for work or studies: *She missed a week of school and had to catch up.*
- **keep up (with somebody)** = stay at the same speed or level: *He cycles so fast that I can't keep up with him.* / *Your marks are excellent — keep it up!*

## B starting things: set up, take up, fix up

| Phrasal verb | Used with | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **set up** | a company, an organisation, a website, a system | start / establish it — *Two friends set up a small design agency.* |
| **take up** | a hobby, a sport, an activity | start doing it — *After retiring, my uncle took up painting.* |
| **fix up** | a meeting, an appointment | arrange it — *Can we fix up a time to talk next week?* |

## C growing: grow up, bring up

- **grow up** = become an adult / spend your childhood: *She was born in Lisbon but grew up in Toronto.*
- **bring up** a child = raise, look after and educate a child: *They brought up four children on a very small income.* / *I was brought up to say please and thank you.*

Contrast: children **grow up**; parents **bring** children **up**.

## D cleaning: clean up, clear up, tidy up, wash up

- **clean up / clear up / tidy up** (something) = make a place clean or tidy: *Who's going to clear up after the party?* / *Tidy your room up before your grandparents arrive.*
- **wash up** = wash the dishes after a meal: *You cooked, so I'll wash up.* (or *do the washing-up*)

## E other verbs + up

| Phrasal verb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **end up** somewhere / **end up doing** something | be in that place or situation in the end, often unexpectedly | *We missed the last train and ended up walking home.* / *He studied law but ended up as a chef.* |
| **give up** / **give** something **up** | stop trying / stop doing something | *The puzzle was hard, but I didn't give up.* / *She gave up smoking two years ago.* |
| **make up** something / **be made up of** | form, constitute / consist of | *Women make up 60% of the staff.* / *The team is made up of six engineers.* |
| **take up** space/time | occupy, use | *This sofa takes up half the room.* / *Commuting takes up two hours of my day.* |
| **turn up / show up** | arrive, appear | *Twenty people were invited, but only eight showed up.* |
| **use** something **up** | use all of it so none is left | *We've used up all the milk — can you buy some?* |

Note that **take up** has two meanings (start a hobby; occupy space or time), and that **end up** is followed by a place, an adjective/noun, or an **-ing** form (❌ *ended up to sleep* ✅ *ended up sleeping*).

**See also:** Phrasal verbs 1 (Introduction) → Unit 137; more verbs + up → Units 142, 144.

### Question 27

#### Question
Daniel started the marathon slowly and was a long way behind the leading group. In the last five kilometres he ran much faster and finally reached them. Which sentence describes this?

#### Answer
The correct sentence is *He caught up with the leaders.* **Catch up (with somebody)** means to move faster than the people in front so that you reach them — exactly what Daniel did. **Keep up with** would mean he stayed at the same speed as them throughout, which contradicts "a long way behind"; **came up to** means simply approached (e.g. walked over to somebody), not overtook a gap in a race; **turned up** means arrived or appeared, which does not fit.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. He kept up with the leaders.
2. He caught up with the leaders. (Correct)
3. He came up to the leaders.
4. He turned up with the leaders.

### Question 28

#### Question
Choose the pair that completes both sentences:
(a) After twenty years in banking, Lena _____ her own consultancy.
(b) To get fitter, she also _____ swimming.

#### Answer
The answer is **set up / took up**. You **set up** a company, business or organisation (= start it), so *Lena set up her own consultancy*. You **take up** a sport, hobby or activity (= start doing it), so *she took up swimming*. The verbs are not interchangeable: *took up her own consultancy* is not idiomatic, and *set up swimming* would mean organising swimming as an activity, not starting to swim yourself. *Fix up* is for arranging meetings and appointments.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. took up / set up
2. fixed up / took up
3. set up / took up (Correct)
4. set up / fixed up

### Question 29

#### Question
Which sentence is wrong, and why?

#### Answer
Sentence 3 is wrong: *My grandparents grew me up after my parents moved abroad* should be *My grandparents **brought me up*** … **Grow up** means to become an adult and has no object — children grow up by themselves. To describe what parents or carers do, you need **bring up** (or *raise*). Sentence 1 correctly uses *grew up*; sentence 2 correctly uses the passive *was brought up*; sentence 4 correctly uses *wash up* meaning to do the dishes.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. I grew up in a village with no shops.
2. She was brought up to respect her elders.
3. My grandparents grew me up after my parents moved abroad. (Correct)
4. If you cook tonight, I'll wash up.

### Question 30

#### Question
"We wanted a quiet evening, but a friend phoned, then another, and by midnight there were fifteen people in our flat." Which sentence best completes the story?

#### Answer
The correct option is *We ended up having a party.* **End up doing something** describes the situation you are in at the end, especially when it was not planned. After **end up** the verb takes the **-ing** form, so *ended up to have* is wrong. *Gave up having a party* would mean we stopped trying to have one, the opposite of what happened; *used up a party* is meaningless — **use up** is for supplies that run out.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. We ended up having a party. (Correct)
2. We ended up to have a party.
3. We gave up having a party.
4. We used up a party.

### Question 31

#### Question
Which sentence uses **take up** in the sense of "occupy space or time" rather than "start a hobby"?

#### Answer
Sentence 2 is the one: *The old piano takes up most of the living room* — it occupies space. In sentence 1, *took up the violin* means started learning it as an activity; in sentence 3, *taking up yoga* likewise means starting a new activity. Sentence 4 uses a different verb altogether, *make up* (= constitute), describing what proportion of the class the girls form. **Take up** has both meanings, and only the object (a piece of furniture and a room vs. a hobby) tells you which one is intended.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. My son took up the violin at the age of six.
2. The old piano takes up most of the living room. (Correct)
3. I'm thinking of taking up yoga this winter.
4. Girls make up two thirds of the class.
