# Unit 80 — Noun + noun (a bus driver / a headache)

# Theory

## A Noun + noun = one idea

Two nouns can be put together to name one thing, person or idea. The **first noun works like an adjective**: it tells us *what kind* of thing the second noun is.

| noun + noun | meaning |
|---|---|
| a taxi driver | a person who drives a taxi |
| road tax | tax you pay for using the road |
| the town hall | the hall of the town |
| a cherry tree | a tree that produces cherries |
| a Rome hotel | a hotel in Rome |
| my travel plans | plans for my travels |

Because the first noun classifies, you can build sets: *a kitchen table, a kitchen knife, a kitchen window, a kitchen cupboard* (things to do with the kitchen); *money worries, family worries, exam worries* (different kinds of worries).

The first word sometimes ends in **-ing**: *a sleeping bag* (= a bag for sleeping), *a dining room, a washing-up bowl, a running track*.

## B Three or more nouns

Longer chains are possible: *the airport departure lounge*, *the European Athletics Championships*, *a mobile phone charger*. Compare *table tennis* (a game) and *a table tennis bat* (a bat used for that game).

## C One word or two?

Some combinations are written as one word (*toothbrush, weekend, sunglasses, footpath*), others as two (*car park, bus stop, credit card*). There is no reliable rule; when unsure, write **two words**.

## D noun + noun vs noun + of + noun

| noun + noun (the kind of container) | noun of noun (container with contents) |
|---|---|
| a wine glass (may be empty) | a glass of wine (full of wine) |
| a lunch box (may be empty) | a box of lunch — not usual; *a box of sandwiches* (with sandwiches in it) |
| a tea cup | a cup of tea |

## E The first noun is singular

Since the first noun acts as an adjective, it is normally **singular**, even when the meaning is plural: *a car park* (for many cars), *a shoe shop* (sells many shoes), *a toy factory*.

The same applies to measurements used before a noun, which are joined with hyphens:

- a two-hour delay (= a delay that lasts two hours) ❌ *a two-hours delay*
- a fifty-euro note, a six-week course, a ten-kilometre run, three 12-year-old boys

Compare the position:

| before the noun (singular, hyphen) | after the verb (plural) |
|---|---|
| It was a six-week course. | The course lasted six weeks. |
| She has a five-year-old son. | Her son is five years old. |
| It's a three-mile walk. | We walked three miles. |

**See also:** -'s and of … → Unit 81; a week's holiday / three weeks' holiday etc. → Unit 81E.

#### Question
Which phrase correctly names "a person who repairs bicycles"?

#### Answer
The correct phrase is *a bicycle repairer* — noun + noun, where the first noun (*bicycle*) says what kind of repairer. The first noun in such compounds stays **singular** even though the person repairs many bicycles, so *a bicycles repairer* is wrong. *A repairer bicycle* reverses the order and would mean a kind of bicycle. *A repairer of bicycle* needs the plural (*of bicycles*) and is in any case not the compact noun + noun form being tested.

### Question 45

#### Question
What is the difference between *a coffee cup* and *a cup of coffee*?

#### Answer
*A coffee cup* is noun + noun: the first noun classifies the cup — it is a type of cup designed for coffee, and it may be **empty**. *A cup of coffee* uses **of** and refers to a cup **with coffee in it**, i.e. the drink itself. The same contrast holds for *a shopping bag* / *a bag of shopping* and *a wine glass* / *a glass of wine*. Option 1 wrongly claims they are synonyms; option 3 reverses the meanings; option 4 invents a formality distinction.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. They mean exactly the same; *of* is just more formal.
2. *A coffee cup* is a kind of cup (perhaps empty); *a cup of coffee* is a cup containing coffee. (Correct)
3. *A coffee cup* is full of coffee; *a cup of coffee* is an empty cup for coffee.
4. *A cup of coffee* is used only in writing; *a coffee cup* only in speech.

### Question 46

#### Question
Spot the mistake: "It's a forty-minutes drive to the airport, so the journey takes about forty minutes."

#### Answer
The mistake is *a forty-minutes drive*. When a measurement comes **before** a noun it behaves like an adjective: the unit is **singular** and joined with a hyphen — *a forty-minute drive*. After the verb the plural is normal: *the journey takes forty minutes*, which is correct in the sentence. Option 1 wrongly "corrects" the correct part; option 3 removes the hyphen but keeps the plural error; option 4 mis-fixes the structure with *of*.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. *forty minutes* should be *forty-minute*, to match the first half.
2. *a forty-minutes drive* should be *a forty-minute drive*: before a noun the unit is singular. (Correct)
3. *a forty-minutes drive* should be *a forty minutes drive*, without the hyphen.
4. *a forty-minutes drive* should be *a drive of forty-minute*.
