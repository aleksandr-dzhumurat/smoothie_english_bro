# Unit 38 — if I do … and if I did …

# Theory

## A. Two ways of talking about a possibility

Imagine two friends deciding how to get to a festival.

1. Before they decide, Ines says: **If we drive, it will be** quicker. – Driving is a **real possibility**, so she uses **if + present … will**.
2. Later they have decided to go by coach. Ines tells someone: **If we drove, it would be** quicker, but parking is a nightmare. – Now she **knows they are not going to drive**, so she uses **if + past … would**.

| type | form | when |
|---|---|---|
| real / open | **if + present**, **will / can / may** … | the speaker thinks it may well happen |
| unreal / imagined | **if + past**, **would / could / might** … | the speaker thinks it won't happen, or is just imagining |

## B. if + past for things we don't expect to happen

When we talk about something that **will not happen**, or that we **don't expect** to happen, we use **if + past simple**. The meaning is **not past**:

- What **would** you do if a stranger **offered** you a million pounds? (I don't expect this)
- If the office **closed** tomorrow, where **would** everyone work? (it won't close)
- I**'d be** amazed if Leo **turned up** on time. (I expect him to be late)

Compare *if I find* and *if I found*:

- I think I dropped my glove in your car. **If you find** it, **can** you bring it tomorrow? (a real possibility)
- **If you found** a suitcase full of cash, what **would** you do? (imagining)

## C. would goes in the main clause, not the if-clause

We do **not** normally use *would* in the *if*-part:

- I'd panic if the lights **went out**. ❌ *if the lights would go out*
- If we **left** at six, we'd avoid the traffic. ❌ *If we would leave*

In the **other part** of the sentence we use **would ('d) / wouldn't**:

- If I **had** a bigger kitchen, I**'d cook** far more.
- I'm not going to text him now. If I **texted** him at 2 a.m., he **wouldn't** be pleased.
- What **would** you say if your boss **asked** you to relocate?

**Could** and **might** are also possible in the main clause:

- If I **won** the competition, I **might** spend the prize on a trip. (= it's possible I would)
- If the rain **stopped**, we **could** eat outside. (= we would be able to)

Rosa is a keen runner and often sees her neighbour Ben at the park. A friend of Ben's wants to get a message to him. Which does Rosa say?

#### Answer

"What would happened" is not a correct verb form.

### Question 30

#### Question
Which sentence contains a mistake, and what is it?

#### Answer
"I'd be furious if someone **would scratch** my car" is wrong: *would* is not normally used in the *if*-clause of an unreal condition. The correct sentence is *I'd be furious if someone **scratched** my car*. ### Question 32

#### Question
Read: "If the museum stayed open later, more people could visit after work." What does *could* mean here, and why is the past tense *stayed* used?

#### Answer
*Could* here means "**would be able to**" – it is an alternative to *would* in the main clause of an unreal condition. The past tense *stayed* does not refer to past time; it marks the situation as **imagined or unlikely** (the museum does not, in fact, stay open later). So the sentence is about the present/general situation, not about the past, and it is not describing something that regularly happened.

#### Correct variant
¯\_(ツ)_/¯

#### Variants
1. *Could* means "were able to" in the past, and *stayed* shows the museum used to stay open later.
2. *Could* means "would be able to"; the past tense *stayed* marks an imagined, unlikely situation, not past time. (Correct)
3. *Could* is a polite request, and *stayed* is a mistake – it should be *stays*.
4. *Could* means "might have been able to", and the sentence describes a missed opportunity in the past.
