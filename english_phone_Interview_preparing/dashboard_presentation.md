**Tip 1. Be clear on the purpose of the dashboard**

Start your presentation by telling the audience what problems the dashboard can solve or what questions it can give answers to.

**Tip 2. Don’t assume your audience know your dashboard**

Just because *you* know what the dashboard shows, you can’t expect the same from the other people. Some of the meeting participants may not have the faintest idea what the measures mean or what the different pieces of information on the dashboard are — take your time to explain these things as well as the data you’re showing.

**Useful language**

- The dashboard shows / represents / displays / illustrates …
- The dashboard gives an overview of …

**Tip 3. Guide your audience through the presentation**

Comment on what you’re demonstrating to help your listeners follow the demo. Don’t jump from one section or part of the dashboard to another at great speed.

**Useful language**

- Let’s go through …
- Now, let’s move on to …
- Now let’s take a (closer) look at …
- If we *[select …]*, we’ll *[see that …]*
- We can drill down into *[this category]* to *[explore which items are more popular]*.
- anticipate - предвидеть (example:Try to anticipate some of the possible questions)

**Tip 4. Draw attention to each area you’re discussing**

Direct your audience to where you want them to look and point at the dashboard sections, elements or visuals you want to talk about. Refer to their location on the dashboard so it’s easier for everyone to find them.

**Useful language**

- As you can see, …
- If we hover over *[a point on the graph]*, a tooltip will appear (showing …)
- We can use the filter pane below to …
- Clicking on *[a region]* will filter the dashboard to display *[the data relevant to this region]*.
- across the top / across the bottom (across == from the left to the right)
- at the top / at the bottom
- on the left / on the right

**Tip 5. Take advantage of use cases**

When you’re presenting a dashboard, you’re not trying to explain how it was built. Instead, you want to give examples to demonstrate how the dashboard should be used, and communicate the insights found.

**Tip 6. Present only what your audience needs to know**

Tell different stories to different audiences instead of telling everything to everyone (they probably won’t be interested and certainly won’t remember!)

**Tip 7. Invite questions**

Ask the audience for feedback to confirm or challenge your assumptions.

**Useful language**

- So, this is basically how you can explore this dashboard (to …)
- Now I’ll be happy to hear your feedback and answer your questions.

# Useful language

**Introducing the visual**

- Let’s have a look at …
- It shows / illustrates / represents …
- As you can see from *[this chart],* …

**Talking about trends**

- *[Sales]* dropped / went down *[by 5% / from 9,000 to 5,000]*.
- *[Profits]* fell sharply/slightly.
- We see a (dramatic/sudden/slight) decline/drop in (revenue).
- *[Sales]* increased / went up / rise (by one third / by half).
- *[Profits]* rose sharply/slightly.
- We see a/an (large) increase (decline) in *[numbers]*.
- We can see (there’s) a (clear) upward/downward trend (in the number of accidents).
- Compared to 2019, …

**Talking about percentages and proportions**

- The (vast == ) majority of *[users are satisfied with their experience]*.
- Just under / A little over *[10 percent of the bugs are low priority]*.
- About / roughly *[one third of the bugs are high priority]*.

**Giving a summary/evaluation**

- Overall, we can see that …
- This seems to suggest that …

# **Tips on how to structure a presentation**

The logic and structure of your presentation would greatly depend on the situation and task; however, it’s always a good idea to have at least three parts — opening, body, and closing.

**Tip 1**

At the start of your presentation communicate the goal of the discussion clearly. When giving a longer talk consisting of several parts, it’s worth showing this structure on the agenda slide and briefly outlining it to the audience so they know what to expect. You may also want to tell the audience when you’re going to take their questions (unless everyone already knows that).

**Useful language**

- Hello/Hi everyone. Thank you for making the time to come. (more formal)

Thanks for joining. (less formal)

- Today I’ll be talking about …
- I’ll be happy to answer your questions as we go along.

I’ll leave some time to answer your questions at the end.

**Tip 2**

Think about what sections you need to include in the body of your presentation and how you’re going to move from one to another smoothly. Bridge the gap between ideas and signal the transitions as you speak. This could help your audience follow you.

**Useful language**

- I’d like to start by *[talking about …]*
- Now turning to …
- Now I’d like to dive into …
- Now let’s go through …
- Let’s move on to …
- Let me walk you through …

# **Tips on using storytelling to engage the audience**

**Tip 1**

To tell a good story, you need to know who will be listening to it. Defining your audience will help you identify what kind of message you want to communicate. The leadership team could be more interested in the final outcomes and actionable insights. The technical audience may be more interested in raw data and technical action points. Different audiences might also require different levels of formality.

**Tip 2**

Construct the narrative you will share with your audience. A story usually includes the following main elements:

- The context — the current situation and your reason for telling the story.
- The characters — stakeholders, customers, clients, or other people affected by your story.
- The conflict — a problem discovered in the data, an inefficient process, or a new opportunity.
- The resolution — the key insights and action points that can help the characters solve the problem.

**Tip 3**

Make your story memorable. Using visuals, delivering meaning, engaging your audience with questions, using recaps and summaries could help your audience remember the message you want to communicate.

# Tips for chart presentation

**Tip 1**

At the end of your presentation you could summarize what you’ve discussed, outline some action points, invite questions, and mention follow-up materials if that’s applicable.

**Useful language**

- I guess that about wraps it up.
- If you have any questions, feel free to ask them.
- As a follow-up, I’ll *[send the link to the recording / share the slide deck].*

**Tip 2**

It’s a good idea to try and anticipate the questions that your audience may ask you, but of course you can’t be prepared for all of them.

One strategy is to ask the person to clarify or repeat the question (or even repeat or reformulate the question yourself). This will give you some time to think about the answer.

Don’t be afraid to admit you don’t know or haven’t thought about something. Thank the audience for the question, promise to find the information later or redirect the question to someone who knows better.

Some questions would be too much of a diversion to answer them in front of the whole audience, or they could even be off-topic. Respond politely and suggest postponing such questions until the end of the meeting if there is time, or promise to get back to the person after the presentation.

**Useful language**

- I’m not completely sure but I’ll find that out for you.
- I’ll get back to you on that.
- *[Nick]*, do you have an answer to that?
- Thank you for bringing that up.
- That’s a little outside the scope of *[our presentation / what we wanted to talk about]*. Do you mind if we discuss it after *[the demo]*?
